Chapter 15
Institutional Capital Inside ETFs: Index Products Have Their Own Personality


When many investors first encounter ETFs,

they often develop a misconception:

ETFs are simpler than individual stocks.

They believe that individual stocks carry earnings risk,

management risk,

valuation risk,

and news risk.

By contrast,

an ETF is a basket of assets—

more diversified,

more transparent,

and generally less volatile.

As long as the overall market direction is roughly correct,

they assume it is difficult to go seriously wrong.

There is some truth to that belief.

ETFs are indeed more diversified than individual stocks.

They do help investors reduce company-specific risk.

And they are excellent tools for sector allocation,

index investing,

and long-term portfolio management.

However,

if that leads you to believe ETFs have no emotions,

no institutional positioning,

and no Market Structure,

then you are mistaken.

An ETF is still a tradable asset.

Where there is a price,

there are buyers and sellers.

Where there are buyers and sellers,

there are capital inflows and outflows.

Where money flows,

there will be trends,

Support,

Resistance,

Breakouts,

False Breakouts,

and Relative Strength.

ETFs are not without personality.

Their personality simply does not come from a single company.

It comes from the collective opinion of institutional capital toward a particular sector,

theme, or index.

Ethan Carter truly understood this

through a healthcare technology ETF that I will call the Euro HealthTech ETF.

The ETF tracked the European healthcare technology and life sciences equipment sector.

Its holdings included medical device manufacturers,

diagnostic service providers,

laboratory automation companies,

and several digital healthcare software businesses.

At that time,

the market was filled with contradictions.

Technology stocks had rallied rapidly,

yet valuation pressure was becoming increasingly severe.

Bank stocks benefited from higher interest rates,

but slowing economic growth raised concerns about bad loans.

Consumer stocks were becoming increasingly divided,

while luxury goods were beginning to display topping behavior.

The clean energy sector struggled to sustain its rebounds,

and investors remained uncertain.

Meanwhile,

the healthcare technology sector was remarkably quiet.

There were no major headlines.

No explosive rallies.

No trending stories dominating financial media.

Yet it possessed one important characteristic:

It had stopped falling.

II. Sector Rotation Is More Than Just a Buzzword

Ethan quickly compared the Euro HealthTech ETF with several other sector ETFs.

He created a watchlist containing four funds:

       Euro HealthTech ETF
       Euro Luxury Consumer ETF
       Euro Clean Energy ETF
       Euro Banks ETF

Over the previous three months,

their performance had diverged significantly.

The Luxury Consumer ETF had been one of the market's strongest performers,

but it had recently begun consolidating near its highs,

with several of its major holdings forming Double Top patterns.

The Clean Energy ETF remained weak.

Its rebounds lacked conviction,

and its lows were not moving higher.

The Banks ETF had become increasingly volatile,

caught between the positive effects of higher interest rates

and the growing risks associated with a slowing economy.

The HealthTech ETF had not delivered a spectacular rally,

but its lows remained stable,

and its Relative Strength continued to improve.

Looking at the comparison chart,

Ethan said,

"The market isn't rising as one.

Capital is simply moving from one sector to another."

"Exactly. That is sector rotation."

Many investors hear the phrase sector rotation

and assume it is merely a macroeconomic concept.

Money moves from technology to consumer stocks.

From growth to value.

From cyclical sectors to defensive sectors.

From expensive sectors to cheaper ones.

Those ideas are certainly useful,

but unless they can be seen directly on a chart,

they remain little more than abstract theories.

True sector rotation

must be reflected through Price Action and capital behavior.

Strong sectors hold their gains during pullbacks.

Weak sectors fail to sustain their rebounds.

Emerging leaders break out first.

Former leaders begin stalling near their highs.

And Relative Strength changes direction.

These are the real signs of sector rotation.

III. What Should You Look for When an ETF Breaks Out?

Over the following two weeks,

the Euro HealthTech ETF continued trading between 48 and 54.

Meanwhile,

its Relative Strength line continued rising.

The broader market experienced several pullbacks,

yet the ETF never broke below 48.

Several of the sector's largest constituent stocks also began stabilizing.

Although Trading Volume did not surge,

it gradually increased on advancing days

and contracted noticeably during declines.

This was a classic example of a gradual institutional accumulation structure.

Unlike many individual stocks,

ETFs rarely explode upward overnight.

Because an ETF represents an entire basket of securities,

institutional allocation tends to occur more gradually.

Its movement resembles a large ship changing course.

It cannot turn as quickly as a small boat,

but once its direction has changed,

the trend often lasts much longer.

Each day,

Ethan monitored three indicators.

First,

whether the ETF continued holding above Support.

Second,

whether its Relative Strength line continued improving.

Third,

whether the sector's major constituent stocks were strengthening together.

"That's exactly right," I said.

"You cannot evaluate an ETF by looking only at the ETF itself.

You must also examine its internal structure."

If an ETF breaks out

but only one or two heavyweight constituents are responsible for the advance,

while the majority of holdings fail to participate,

An ETF is a basket.

A stronger basket should never depend on only one or two of its largest apples.

IV. A Retest Reveals More Than the Breakout Itself

On the day after the breakout,

the Euro HealthTech ETF moved modestly higher.

On the third day,

instead of continuing its advance,

it pulled back toward 54.

That was perfectly normal.

A Retest following a breakout

is the market's way of testing whether participants truly accept the new price level.

Ethan focused on Trading Volume.

During the pullback,

volume contracted noticeably.

The ETF briefly declined to 53.90,

but closed at 54.30.

That indicated that the former Resistance zone

was beginning to act as new Support.

On the fourth day,

the ETF resumed its advance,

moving back above 55.

This was a textbook example of a healthy ETF breakout confirmation.

Ethan said,

"If this were an individual stock,

I might worry that it wasn't strong enough.

But for an ETF,

this behavior actually seems more normal."

"Why?"

"Because ETFs generally experience smaller price swings.

A breakout doesn't necessarily lead to an immediate surge.

What really matters

"Exactly."

ETF trading cannot be judged by the same standards used for highly speculative individual stocks.

A stock may explode higher immediately after a breakout.

An ETF,

however,

may simply continue advancing at a steady pace.

Its advantage lies elsewhere.

Once institutional asset allocation shifts decisively toward a sector,

the resulting trend is often more stable,

making ETFs especially well suited for portfolio management.

That evening,

the topic of Ethan's whiteboard lesson was:

Sector Bases and Relative Strength.

The essence of ETF analysis

is not simply determining whether the ETF is rising.

You must also evaluate:

       Whether it is outperforming the broader market.
       Whether it is outperforming other sectors.
       Whether its constituent stocks are improving together.

Although ETFs appear simple,

I. What Is a Sector Base?

A sector base

forms after an industry or investment theme has endured a prolonged decline,

when prices stop falling

and gradually establish a bottoming structure.

A typical sector base usually displays several characteristics:

       The sector ETF stops making new lows.
       The Support zone is tested repeatedly and continues to hold.
       Trading Volume gradually stabilizes near the bottom.
       The sector's leading constituent stocks stop declining.
       Relative Strength versus the broader market begins improving.
       Eventually, price breaks above the trading range's Resistance.

Sector bases differ from the bases formed by individual stocks.

A single stock can reverse rapidly following a company-specific event.

An entire sector,

however,

usually requires many stocks to improve together

before a sustainable trend can emerge.

As a result,




                                               - 324 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 333 / 686>
sector bases generally take longer to develop.

Yet they often carry greater significance.

Once institutional capital begins flowing back into an entire sector,

it can lift many stocks simultaneously—

not just a single company.



II. What Is Relative Strength?

Relative Strength

measures the performance of one asset compared with another benchmark.

For example:

       The Euro HealthTech ETF relative to the broader European stock market.
       The healthcare technology ETF relative to the luxury consumer ETF.
       A medical equipment company relative to the healthcare technology ETF.

If an ETF has not risen dramatically,

yet consistently outperforms the broader market,

it suggests that it is becoming either more resilient

or a new market leader.

Improving Relative Strength generally appears in three ways:

       The ETF declines less when the market falls.
       It rises more when the market rallies.
       During market consolidations, its lows continue moving higher.

These are clear signs

that institutional capital is gradually becoming more constructive toward the sector.

III. Why Is Relative Strength So Important?

Because institutional capital is always making comparisons.

A portfolio manager does not simply ask,

        "Will this sector go up?"

They also ask,

        "Is this sector more deserving of capital than the others?"

Asset allocation is fundamentally a process of relative selection.




                                              - 326 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 335 / 686>
If technology is outperforming,

capital may rotate into technology.

If defensive sectors prove more resilient,

capital may shift toward defensive industries.

If energy companies begin delivering stronger earnings,

capital may flow into the energy sector.

If highly valued sectors begin weakening,

investors may rotate toward industries with more stable cash flows.

For that reason,

a sector's transition from weakness to strength

usually appears first in its Relative Strength.

The breakout in absolute price

comes later as confirmation.



IV. The Four-Step ETF Analysis Process

From that day forward,

I required Ethan to analyze every ETF using the following four-step process.

Step One: Evaluate the Price Structure

Determine whether the ETF is in an uptrend,

a downtrend,

or a bottoming phase.

Identify its Support,

Resistance,

trading range,

or any recognizable base pattern.

Step Two: Evaluate Relative Strength




                                                  - 327 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 336 / 686>
Is the ETF outperforming the broader market,

or underperforming it?

If price has not yet advanced

but Relative Strength is already improving,

it may be becoming a new institutional favorite.

Step Three: Evaluate Internal Breadth

Are the ETF's largest constituent stocks improving together?

Are an increasing number of holdings moving above their 20-day and 50-day Moving Averages?

Or is the ETF's advance being driven by only a handful of heavyweight components?

Step Four: Evaluate the Breakout and the Retest

Has the ETF broken above a major Resistance level?

Did Trading Volume improve during the breakout?

Afterward,

did the Retest successfully hold above the breakout level?

Case Review

Why Was the Euro HealthTech ETF Worth Watching?

Now,

let us return to the Euro HealthTech ETF.

If you looked only at its price,

it appeared rather ordinary.

It simply traded sideways between 48 and 54.

However,

once Relative Strength and the broader market environment were taken into account,

the picture became much more meaningful.

First, during periods of market volatility, the ETF stopped making new lows.

Second, its Support level held successfully on multiple occasions.

Third, its Relative Strength line versus the broader market began rising.

Fourth, while several previously popular sectors weakened, healthcare technology gradually
became more resilient.

Fifth, its leading constituent stocks progressively moved above their short-term Moving Averages.

Sixth, the ETF eventually broke above 54 on expanding Trading Volume.

Seventh, after the breakout, the Retest occurred on lighter Trading Volume and successfully held
above the breakout level.

Together, these signals suggested that the move was not simply a short-term rebound.

Instead, it was likely the beginning of a new wave of institutional sector allocation.

The correct trading approach

was not to establish a large position

the moment Relative Strength began improving.

Instead, the proper sequence was:

       Add the ETF to the watchlist.
       Wait for price to break above the trading range.
       Confirm that Trading Volume supports the breakout.
       Verify that internal market breadth is improving.
       Wait for the Retest to hold successfully.
       Then decide whether to include the ETF as part of the portfolio allocation.

ETFs are particularly well suited to systematic portfolio management.

They may not always produce the market's most exciting rallies,

but they can help direct capital toward the sectors with the greatest institutional strength.

Chapter Trading Rules

That day,

I asked Ethan to write down six trading rules.

First, an ETF is not emotionless. It reflects institutional capital's attitude toward an entire basket
of assets.

Second, ETF analysis should never rely solely on absolute price. Relative Strength must also be
evaluated.

Third, sector bottoms often appear first through resilience during market declines, then through
improving Relative Strength, and only afterward through a confirmed price breakout.

Fourth, an ETF breakout should be supported by improving internal breadth, not driven by only a
few heavyweight holdings.

Fifth, whether price successfully holds the breakout level after a Retest provides important
evidence of market acceptance.

Sixth, ETFs are best approached from a portfolio allocation perspective rather than through
emotional chasing of short-term price movements.

Then I added one final sentence.

         An individual stock tells you the story of one company. An ETF tells you
         where institutional capital is choosing to go.

Ethan carefully copied that sentence into his notebook.

Later,

he added another line beneath it.

         Follow leadership, not noise.
