Chapter 4
The First Research Report: Fundamentals Are Not a Master Key
The first time Ethan Carter truly felt defeated was not because he lost money chasing a breakout,
nor because he failed to understand a sudden market decline.

It was because he had done something correctly—seriously and diligently—yet the market still
refused to reward him.

That was even more painful than making a mistake.

If someone loses money because they impulsively chase a rising stock, they can at least admit
they acted too hastily.

If someone loses money because they failed to place a stop loss, they can at least admit they
lacked discipline.

If someone loses money because they misread a chart pattern, they can at least go back and
improve their technical analysis.

But if someone spends an entire week researching a company—reading its annual reports,
building financial models, comparing valuation multiples with peers, and analyzing the industry's
long-term potential—only to arrive at a conclusion that appears completely logical while the
market shows no interest whatsoever, a young research analyst inevitably begins to question
something:

Does serious research even matter?

It was with exactly this confusion that Ethan received his first official research assignment.

It was a Friday afternoon.

For the first time in days, the skies over London had cleared. Sunlight streamed through the glass
curtain walls, casting soft reflections across the carpet and along the edges of the trading screens.
The office was quieter than usual, carrying the familiar fatigue that often settles over the market
on Friday afternoons. Traders were reducing unnecessary positions, research analysts were
organizing their notes from the week, and portfolio managers were deciding whether to reduce
risk exposure before the weekend.

Ethan sat at his desk with three empty coffee cups lined up beside him.

On his screen were the company's financial statements from the past five years, accompanied by
several industry research reports.

For the purpose of this story, I will refer to the company as NordicMed.

NordicMed was a European medical device company specializing in high-end diagnostic imaging
equipment and surgical assistance systems. It was not the kind of stock discussed every day on

retail investor forums, nor was it the type of speculative stock that could suddenly surge because
of a single news headline. Instead, it belonged to a category of companies that institutional
investors loved to study but the general public rarely paid much attention to.

Its revenue had grown steadily.

Its gross margin had remained consistently high.

Its cash flow was healthy.

Its debt ratio was relatively low.

Management had demonstrated a strong execution record over many years.

The industry itself was supported by the long-term demand created by an aging population.

From a fundamental perspective, NordicMed was unquestionably a high-quality company.

Ethan found it fascinating.

Over the previous several days, he had devoted almost all of his energy to studying this single
company. He read its latest earnings report, organized the management conference call transcript,
compared valuation multiples across competitors, and even built a simplified discounted cash
flow model.

It was the first time since joining the Research Department that he had independently completed a
full company analysis.

He took it seriously.

Perhaps a little too seriously.

I could tell that he hoped this report would prove that he was capable of much more than reading
charts. He wanted to demonstrate that he truly understood businesses, industries, and financial
statements—that he could produce a research report with genuine depth.

Young research analysts often have this ambition.

They do not want to be seen merely as people who stare at candlestick charts all day. They want
to understand companies, industries, and corporate finance. They want to write reports that people
consider insightful.

There is nothing wrong with that ambition.

But if someone believes that simply because they have written an excellent research report, the
market must follow their conclusions, that ambition becomes another kind of danger.

II. A Good Company Does Not Necessarily Mean a Good Stock

I led Ethan into the conference room and projected NordicMed's price chart onto the large screen.

It was not an attractive chart.

The stock was clearly in a downtrend.

Every rebound had failed to exceed the previous swing high.

The short-term moving averages were pressing down above the price.

Volume expanded during declines but contracted during rebounds.

Although the stock was approaching a major long-term support area, there was still no clear
evidence that buyers were stepping in.

From a fundamental perspective, NordicMed might indeed have been becoming increasingly
attractive.

But from the perspective of price structure, the market had not yet provided any evidence that
buyers had regained control.

I turned to Ethan.

"Do you think the market doesn't know this is a high-quality company?"

He frowned slightly.

"It probably does."

"Do institutional investors not know that its cash flow is healthy?"

"They do."



                                               - 66 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 75 / 686>
"Do analysts not know that its valuation is below its historical average?"

"They do."

"Then why is the stock still falling?"

He had no answer.

I continued,

"Because the market isn't trading how good the company used to be.

The market is trading whether expectations for its future are still being revised downward."

One of the easiest things to overlook in fundamental investing is this:

Even a great company can suffer from changing expectations.

A great company whose expectations have become excessively optimistic can still experience a
significant decline.

An average company whose expectations have become excessively pessimistic can still rise
sharply.

A stock price is not a simple vote on whether a company is good or bad.

It is an ongoing process through which the market continuously revalues future expectations.

NordicMed's problem was not that it had become a poor company.

Its problem was that the market feared it might no longer be as good as before.

Those are two very different things.

I walked to the whiteboard and wrote three sentences:

Good Company ≠ Good Stock

Good Stock ≠ Good Entry

Good Entry Requires Confirmation

A good company does not necessarily make a good stock.

A good stock does not necessarily provide a good entry.

A good entry requires confirmation.

III. The Market Is Not a Valuation Spreadsheet

The following Monday, NordicMed announced a modest order update.

The order data came in slightly above market expectations.

Ethan arrived at the office early that morning.

It was obvious that he expected the market to respond positively.

Before the opening bell, he displayed NordicMed's chart on one side of his screen and placed his
research report beside it. Although he said nothing, his actions made his expectations clear:

He wanted the stock to rise.

One of the easiest mistakes for research analysts to make is believing that once they have written
a convincing report, the market should follow its script.

At 9:00 a.m., NordicMed opened 2.6% higher.

A barely noticeable smile flashed across Ethan's face.

During the first fifteen minutes, the stock continued climbing, reaching an intraday gain of nearly
4%.

"The market is finally recognizing it," he said.

I remained silent.

After 10:00 a.m., the gains began to fade.

By 10:30 a.m., the stock was up only 1.5%.

At 11:00 a.m., it had almost turned negative.

By midday, NordicMed was down 0.8%.

Confusion slowly appeared on Ethan's face.

"Weren't the order numbers better than expected?"

"They were," I replied.

"Then why is the stock falling?"

"Because the market may believe that's still not enough."

"But the data shows demand isn't as weak as people feared."

"Correct.

Especially when the market has already priced in the news beforehand, or when investors remain

This is one of the most painful realities of fundamental investing.

You may be correct about the company, yet completely wrong about when the market is willing
to recognize its value.

You may correctly conclude that the stock is inexpensive over the long term, yet still be unable to
prevent it from becoming even cheaper in the short term.

You may correctly judge that bad news has already been fully reflected in the price, but the
market itself may still need weeks—or even months—before it reaches the same conclusion.

Successful investing is not simply about discovering value before everyone else.

It is also about recognizing when capital is finally willing to acknowledge that value.

This statement is easily misunderstood.

It does not mean that fundamentals are unimportant.

On the contrary, fundamentals are extremely important.

If you know nothing about the underlying business and look only at charts, it is very easy to
mistake a short-term price fluctuation for a long-term investment opportunity.

If you do not understand the industry or the company's earning power, it becomes almost
impossible to judge its long-term potential.

But something else is equally important:

Strong fundamentals can never replace proper entry timing.

One of the most common losses in the market comes from buying a good company and then

You tell yourself:

"The company hasn't changed."

"The valuation has become even cheaper."

Sometimes, that is true.

But sometimes the company's quality is actually deteriorating.

Sometimes the market's valuation multiple has permanently shifted lower.

Sometimes the market no longer assigns premium valuations to that entire sector.

Sometimes you are correct about the long-term story, yet still suffer a drawdown so severe that
you cannot emotionally or financially withstand it.

That is why fundamental analysis must always be combined with price structure.



I. Why Can Great Companies Still Decline?

There are several common reasons why even outstanding companies can experience significant
declines.

1. The Valuation Was Too High

Even an exceptional company can suffer a sharp decline if its valuation has become excessively
expensive.



                                              - 75 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 84 / 686>
The moment its growth slows even slightly, investors may dramatically lower the price they are
willing to pay.

The market does not evaluate companies based solely on quality.

It also evaluates price.

Even the best company can become a poor investment if you pay too much for it.

2. Expectations Were Too High

Sometimes the market has already priced in years of future growth.

When actual results turn out to be merely good rather than far better than expected, the stock may
still decline.

3. Industry Cycles Change

A company itself may remain excellent, yet if its industry enters a weaker phase, capital may
reduce valuations across the entire sector.

4. Capital Rotates Between Styles

When the market rotates from growth stocks into value stocks, or from high-valuation sectors into
defensive sectors, even outstanding companies can experience heavy selling pressure.

5. The Price Structure Has Not Yet Been Confirmed

Even if the company's fundamentals begin improving, institutional investors may still have not
reached a consensus.

The stock may remain trapped in a downtrend while the market slowly completes its bottoming
process.

Price needs time to build a base, transfer ownership, and complete confirmation.

II. How Does Price Structure Confirm the Fundamentals?

When a company's fundamentals begin improving, the stock rarely enters a sustained uptrend
immediately.

Instead, the process usually unfolds in several stages.

Stage One: The Decline Begins to Slow

Most of the bad news has already been reflected in the price.

The stock continues falling, but the pace of the decline begins to moderate.

Stage Two: A Trading Range Forms



                                               - 77 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 86 / 686>
The market becomes divided.

Some investors continue selling.

Others quietly begin accumulating shares.

Price starts moving sideways within a relatively narrow range.

Stage Three: Volume Begins to Change

If institutional investors are gradually accumulating positions, trading volume often begins
increasing modestly—even if price itself has not yet moved significantly.

Stage Four: Price Breaks Above Key Resistance

The stock breaks above its downtrend line, the upper boundary of its trading range, or a major
moving average.

Stage Five: The Retest Holds

After the breakout, price pulls back but successfully holds above the breakout level.

This demonstrates that the market has begun accepting higher prices.

Only at this stage does the fundamental thesis begin transforming into a confirmed price trend.



III. The Relationship Between Fundamentals and Technical Analysis

Many investors enjoy debating a familiar question:

Which is more important—fundamental analysis or technical analysis?

In my opinion, the question itself is flawed.

Fundamental analysis answers one question:

Does this asset possess long-term value?

Technical analysis answers another:

Has the market begun recognizing that value?

Capital flow analysis answers yet another:

Is real money actively pushing the price higher?

Sentiment analysis answers a different question:

Has this trade become overcrowded, or is risk still being underestimated?



                                                - 78 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 87 / 686>
These four dimensions are not substitutes for one another.

They validate one another.

When fundamentals are strong, the price has completed a confirmed breakout, volume supports
the move, and market sentiment has not yet become overheated, the opportunity is generally of
much higher quality.

If fundamentals are strong but price remains trapped in a downtrend, volume shows no
meaningful improvement, and market sentiment remains overwhelmingly pessimistic, the stock
may deserve a place on your watchlist—but not necessarily in your portfolio.

Why Couldn't NordicMed Be Bought Immediately?

Now let's return to NordicMed.

The overall direction of Ethan's fundamental research was not wrong.

The company did indeed possess several attractive characteristics:

       Stable revenue growth.
       Healthy profit margins.
       Strong cash flow.
       Long-term demand supported by its industry.
       A valuation below its historical average.

However, from a trading perspective, several critical conditions were still missing.

First, the stock remained in a clear downtrend.

Second, every rebound had failed to break above the previous swing high.

Third, trading volume showed no evidence of sustained institutional buying.

Fourth, the stock had not reclaimed any major moving averages.

Fifth, the market's reaction to the improved order data was weak.

What does this tell us?

It tells us that while the company's fundamentals may already be becoming attractive,
institutional capital has not yet reached a consensus.

Buying at this stage means purchasing an opinion that the market has not yet confirmed, rather
than participating in an established trend.

That does not necessarily mean the stock cannot be bought.

If an investor follows a long-term value investing strategy, has a sufficiently long investment
horizon, builds positions gradually, and can tolerate substantial drawdowns, accumulating shares
within an undervalued area may be entirely reasonable.

However, if an investor follows a trend-following strategy or a medium-term swing trading
approach, waiting for confirmation of the price structure is generally the more appropriate
decision.

Different investment strategies require different entry criteria.

Ethan's biggest mistake at that time was that he had never clearly defined what kind of investor he
was trying to be.

He used fundamental analysis to justify buying the stock, yet expected the price to rise
immediately.

He relied on a long-term valuation thesis, yet had no long-term holding plan.

He claimed to believe in the company, but never defined what he would do if the market
continued falling.

That was not a research problem.

It was a trading plan problem.



Chapter Trading Rules

That evening, I asked Ethan to write down four rules.

First: A good company does not necessarily provide a good buying opportunity.

Second: A fundamental investment thesis requires confirmation from market price.

Third: A research report answers the question, "Is this company worth paying attention to?" A
trading plan answers the question, "Is now the right time to buy?"

Fourth: If the price structure has not yet been confirmed, the best decision may not be to buy—
but simply to place the stock on your watchlist.

Then I added one more sentence.

Investing is not about randomly choosing a company from a list of good businesses.

Investing is about acting only when a good company, a good price, a good chart structure, and an
attractive risk-reward profile all come together at the same time.

Ethan placed NordicMed on his watchlist instead of adding it to his simulated portfolio.

For him, that represented real progress.

Because in the financial markets, maturity often looks as though nothing is happening.

In reality, having the discipline not to make a bad trade is itself one of the defining qualities of a
professional investor.



Chapter Summary

Ethan Carter's first research report taught him an important lesson:

Being correct in your analysis does not necessarily mean being correct in your trade.

Company research tells us whether a business is fundamentally strong.

Valuation analysis tells us whether the price appears attractive.

Industry research tells us whether long-term growth potential exists.

But making a trading decision requires one additional layer of judgment:

Has the market already begun recognizing that thesis?

Without confirmation from the price structure, even the strongest fundamentals may remain
nothing more than an idea waiting for the right moment.

From this chapter onward, Ethan began classifying stocks into three categories.

Category One: Not worth researching.

Category Two: Worth researching, but not yet ready to buy.

Category Three: Fundamentals, technical structure, capital flow, and risk-reward have begun
aligning, making it appropriate to prepare a trading plan.

That distinction is critically important.

Because true maturity as an investor is not about acting the moment an opportunity appears.

It is about recognizing which opportunities have not yet matured.

In the next chapter, Ethan will begin learning one of the most fundamental—and most frequently
misunderstood—concepts in technical analysis:

Trend.

He will discover that a trend is not simply a line on a chart, nor is it defined by a single day's
price movement.

A trend is the direction that the market continues choosing over time.
