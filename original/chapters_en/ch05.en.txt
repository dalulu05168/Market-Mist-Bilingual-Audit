Chapter 5
The Steering Wheel of Trend: Why the Market Moves with Momentum.


Ethan Carter did not truly learn to respect trends because of an individual stock.

It began with an ETF.

It was not an exciting stock.

There was no surprise acquisition.

No spectacular earnings report.

No heated discussions across social media.

It did not even have a memorable name that retail investors could easily recognize.

It was simply a European technology growth ETF.

For the sake of this story, I will refer to it as EuroTech Growth ETF.

ETFs like this are very common inside research institutions.

Unlike an individual stock, they are far less likely to surge or collapse because of company-
specific news.

Instead, they reflect an entire industry, an investment style, and even shifts in overall market risk
appetite.

In many situations, an ETF is more honest than an individual stock.

A single stock can be distorted by a news headline, an earnings report, or the short-term activity
of a large institution.

An ETF, however, represents a basket of stocks.

It behaves more like a thermometer measuring the market's overall sentiment.

That morning, Ethan was asked to answer a simple question.

Is EuroTech Growth ETF currently in an uptrend, a downtrend, or a sideways trend?

The question sounded easy.

So easy that Ethan felt almost relaxed.

He opened the chart, looked at it for a few seconds, and replied,

"From a short-term perspective, it's moving higher."

The moment the daily chart appeared, his expression changed.

The three-day rally looked like nothing more than a small rebound after a decline.

Within the broader structure, the ETF had spent the past two months failing to break above its

"And now?"

"On the daily chart... it looks more like a rebound within a downtrend."

Now switch to the weekly chart."

The picture changed once again.

Although the ETF had experienced a significant pullback during the previous two months, from a
longer-term perspective it still remained inside the upward channel it had established over the

Price was approaching its long-term trendline as well as a major historical support area.

Ethan frowned.

"So... is it in an uptrend or a downtrend?"

I smiled.

This is the very first question every investor encounters when learning about trends.

The trend you see depends on the timeframe from which you are looking.

On a five-minute chart, the market may resemble a violent storm.

On a daily chart, it may simply be an ordinary pullback.

Beginners are often controlled by short-term timeframes.

They see one hour of strength and conclude that the trend has turned bullish.

They see one day of weakness and conclude that the trend is over.

They see one large bearish candle and immediately want to sell.

They see one large bullish candle and immediately want to chase the move.

They are not analyzing trends.

They are reacting to noise.

I looked at Ethan and said,

"A trend is not determined by whether the market rises or falls on a particular day. A trend is the
direction the market continues choosing over a period of time."

I. Ethan's Confusion: Every Candlestick Looks Like a New Opinion

Ethan returned to his desk and displayed the three charts side by side.

The short-term chart was rising.

"Ask yourself one question first," I replied.

"How long do you intend to hold the position?"

He paused.

The question sounded simple, yet it resolved an enormous amount of confusion.

If you are an intraday trader, the weekly trend only provides background.

Your actual entries and exits should be based primarily on short-term price structure.

If you are a swing trader holding positions for several weeks or several months, the daily trend
becomes far more important, while the hourly chart serves mainly to fine-tune your entry.

If you are a long-term investor with a holding period of one to three years, the weekly and
monthly trends become your primary focus.

Most intraday fluctuations are nothing more than noise.

Many investors become confused simply because they never define their investment timeframe
before analyzing the market.

They buy using a daily-chart thesis but panic and sell because of a five-minute chart.

The true foundation of trend analysis is not the timeframe itself.

II. A Trend Is Not a Line—It Is the Relationship Between Highs and Lows

I projected the daily chart of EuroTech Growth ETF onto the large screen.

During the previous two months, price had certainly not fallen in a straight line.

There had been numerous rebounds along the way.

Some of those rebounds were surprisingly strong.

Within one or two trading sessions, the gains were large enough to convince many investors that
the trend had already reversed.

But structurally, one critical problem remained.

Every rebound peak was lower than the previous rebound peak.

Every decline produced a new low below the previous one.

That is the definition of a downtrend.

I walked to the whiteboard and wrote two English phrases.

Lower High / Lower Low

Then I drew another structure beside it.

Higher High / Higher Low

I turned back toward Ethan.

"A trend is not established simply because price rises on one particular day.

A trend is defined by an entire sequence of highs and lows."

The defining feature of an uptrend is not that the market rises every day.

It is that after each pullback, the new low remains above the previous low, and the next rally
breaks above the previous high.

Likewise, the defining feature of a downtrend is not that the market falls every day.

It is that each rebound fails to exceed the previous high, while every new decline pushes to a
lower low.

Ethan studied the chart for a moment before suddenly saying,

"So... a rebound doesn't necessarily mean a reversal."

"Correct."

"And a decline doesn't necessarily mean the trend has ended."

"Also correct."

That is one of the most important principles in trend analysis.

Within an uptrend, a decline may simply be an ordinary pullback.

Within a downtrend, a rally may simply be a temporary rebound.

range.

Exactly the same price movement can carry completely different meanings depending on the
surrounding trend structure.

III. Why Do Trends Continue?

Ethan then asked an excellent question.

"Why does the market keep moving in the direction of the trend? Why doesn't it immediately
reverse after rising or falling?"

I like questions like that.




                                            - 89 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 98 / 686>
Because they show that he had begun shifting his focus from "What does the pattern look like?"
to "Why does it behave this way?"

I answered,

"There are three forces behind every sustained trend."

The first is capital momentum.

When an asset begins to rise, early buyers make money.

As performance improves, additional capital starts flowing in.

Fund managers may increase their allocations.

Quantitative models may generate buy signals.

Trend-following traders may enter after breakout confirmations.

Media coverage attracts even more attention.

In other words, rising prices themselves attract additional buyers.

That is how a trend reinforces itself.

The second is strengthening expectations.

A rising trend changes the market's expectations about the future.

When prices continue climbing, investors become more willing to believe positive news.

When prices continue falling, investors become more willing to believe negative news.

Exactly the same earnings report may be interpreted as "steady growth" during an uptrend, yet
dismissed as "growth is no longer fast enough" during a downtrend.

A trend influences how information is interpreted.

The third is portfolio repositioning.

Large institutions rarely complete their buying or selling in a single transaction.

When they decide to increase exposure to a sector, accumulation may continue for weeks or even
months.

Every pullback attracts additional institutional buying.

Every breakout encourages fresh capital to follow.

This is why the behavior of large institutions often leaves behind recognizable trend patterns.

Ethan nodded thoughtfully.

It's the result of capital and expectations repeatedly making the same choice."

Professor's Whiteboard Session

Trend Structure

Trend is one of the most fundamental concepts in technical analysis.

Yet the more fundamental a concept is, the more easily it is misunderstood.

Many people believe that a trend simply means prices are rising or falling.

In reality, a trend is not defined by a single day's price movement, nor is it represented by a line
drawn arbitrarily on a chart.

A trend is a directional price structure that develops over time.



I. Uptrend

The defining characteristic of an uptrend is straightforward.

Price forms a sequence of Higher Highs and Higher Lows.

In technical analysis, this structure is commonly referred to as:

Higher Highs and Higher Lows

This means that each advance pushes above the previous high, while every pullback finds support
above the previous low.

What does this tell us?

Buyers are willing to purchase at increasingly higher prices.

Sellers are no longer able to force the market back into lower price ranges.

The market's accepted value for the asset is gradually moving higher.

Within an uptrend, a pullback is not necessarily a warning sign.

If trading volume contracts during the pullback and price remains above the previous low or a key
support level, the decline may simply represent a healthy pause within the ongoing trend.



II. Downtrend

The defining characteristic of a downtrend is equally clear.


                                                - 96 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 105 / 686>
Price forms a sequence of Lower Highs and Lower Lows.

This structure is commonly known as:

Lower Highs and Lower Lows

Each rebound fails to exceed the previous high.

Each new decline pushes to a lower low.

What does this indicate?

Sellers continue accepting lower prices in order to exit their positions.

Buyers lack sufficient demand to absorb the selling pressure.

The market's accepted value for the asset is gradually moving lower.

Within a downtrend, a rebound does not automatically create an opportunity.

If the rebound occurs on declining volume and cannot break above the previous high or an
important resistance level, it is likely nothing more than a temporary pause within the broader
decline.

III. Sideways Trend

A sideways trend is also known as a trading range.

Instead of producing a consistent series of higher highs or lower lows, price fluctuates within a
relatively fixed range.

The upper boundary acts as resistance.

The lower boundary acts as support.

This type of market is often the most difficult environment for trend-following traders.

Each rally appears to be a breakout.

Each decline appears to be a breakdown.

Yet in many cases, price simply returns to the middle of the range.

Within a sideways market, the most important task is not predicting the next candlestick.

The objective is to identify the boundaries of the range.

Is price approaching the upper boundary?

Is it approaching the lower boundary?

Does trading volume support a genuine breakout?

Can price remain above the range after the breakout?

If price cannot successfully break beyond the range, a new trend has not yet been established.



IV. Three Steps to Identifying a Trend

Later, I asked Ethan to follow the same three-step process every time he analyzed a trend.

Step One: Define Your Trading Timeframe

Are you trading intraday?

Swing trading?

Or investing for the long term?


                                               - 98 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 107 / 686>
Different timeframes produce different trend definitions.

Never use a five-minute chart to invalidate a weekly trend.

And never allow a weekly trend to ignore a confirmed breakdown on the daily chart.

Step Two: Identify the Key Highs and Lows

Do not begin by drawing trendlines.

Start by identifying the structure.

Where is the most recent rebound high?

Where is the most recent pullback low?

Has price broken above the previous high?

Has it fallen below the previous low?

The relationship between highs and lows is far more important than any individual candlestick.

Step Three: Combine Price Structure with Volume and Key Levels

Trend analysis is not based solely on price.

During an uptrend:

       Does trading volume increase during breakouts?
       Does volume contract during pullbacks?

During a downtrend:

       Does volume expand during breakdowns?
       Do rebounds appear weak and lack conviction?

Only by combining price structure, trading volume, and key support and resistance levels can
trend analysis become truly reliable.

Case Review

Trend Analysis of EuroTech Growth ETF

Now let's return to EuroTech Growth ETF.

Why was it such an effective example for learning trend analysis?

Because it displayed different trend conditions across different timeframes.

On the short-term chart, the ETF had rebounded for three consecutive days, making it easy to
believe that the market had turned bullish.




                                              - 100 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 109 / 686>
On the daily chart, however, it remained in a downtrend because the rebound had failed to break
above the previous swing high, while each successive low continued moving lower.

On the weekly chart, price was approaching long-term support but had not yet broken the broader
long-term uptrend.

What does this teach us?

Trend analysis cannot be based on a single chart alone.

If Ethan were a short-term trader, he could monitor whether the three-day rebound continued.

However, he would also need to recognize that this remained only a short-term opportunity
within a broader daily downtrend.

If Ethan were a swing trader, he should wait for the daily structure to improve—for example, by
breaking above the previous rebound high or by establishing a Higher Low before advancing
again.

If Ethan were a long-term investor, he should focus on whether the weekly support remained
intact, rather than changing his opinion every time the daily chart fluctuated.

That is the difference between professional analysis and emotional decision-making.

Emotional thinking always seeks a simple answer.

Buy. / Sell. / Bullish. / Bearish.

Professional analysis begins by asking a different set of questions.

Which timeframe am I analyzing?

Which trading strategy am I using?

Has the trend actually been confirmed?

Where is the risk?

If the market structure changes, should my opinion change as well?

Chapter Trading Rules

That day, I asked Ethan to write down four rules about trend analysis.

First: A trend is not determined by a single day's price movement. It is defined by the relationship
between a series of highs and lows.

Second: An uptrend is characterized by Higher Highs and Higher Lows. A downtrend is
characterized by Lower Highs and Lower Lows.

Third: Every trend analysis must begin by identifying the appropriate timeframe.

True trend analysis is not about asking whether the market will rise or fall today.

It is about asking whether the market is consistently accepting higher prices—or consistently

Chapter Summary

A trend has its own timeframe.
