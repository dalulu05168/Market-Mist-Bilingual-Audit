Chapter 9
The Temperature of Moving Averages: How Market Cost Becomes the Track of a
Trend


Ethan Carter's initial understanding of moving averages was much like that of most investors.

He believed that a moving average was simply a form of support.

If price stayed above the moving average, the stock was strong.

If price fell below the moving average, the stock was weak.

If price pulled back to the moving average, it was a buying opportunity.

If the moving average turned upward, a new trend had begun.

None of these ideas is entirely wrong.

But they are far too simplistic.

So simplistic, in fact, that they can become dangerous.

Moving averages can certainly help us observe trends, and they can also help us understand the
market's average cost basis. But if an investor treats them as automatic buy-and-sell buttons, it
won't take long before the market repeatedly moves back and forth across the moving average,
wearing down both the investor's confidence and their trading account.

The first time Ethan truly understood moving averages was not because he watched a stock ride
above one during a strong advance.

It was because he witnessed a stock that appeared to be making a normal pullback to its moving
average, only to break below it and trigger a deterioration in market structure.

I will refer to that stock as Atlas Mobility.

Atlas Mobility was a European transportation technology company specializing in intelligent
logistics systems, fleet management software, and electric commercial vehicle scheduling
platforms. It had once been a highly popular growth stock, producing a smooth and well-defined
uptrend throughout the previous year.

One characteristic stood out clearly.

The stock repeatedly pulled back to its 20-day Moving Average before bouncing higher.

Each pullback held above the 20-day Moving Average without any meaningful breakdown.

Each time price reclaimed the short-term moving averages, the uptrend resumed.

Trading volume expanded moderately during advances and gradually contracted during pullbacks.

This type of price action makes it very easy for investors to develop a fixed belief:

The 20-day Moving Average is support.

Ethan believed exactly that.

That morning, he brought me the chart of Atlas Mobility.

"Professor, the stock has pulled back to its 20-day Moving Average again."

"And then?"

"The last several pullbacks all bounced from here. This might be another buying opportunity."

I looked at the chart without saying anything immediately.

At first glance, Atlas Mobility did appear to be making nothing more than a routine pullback.

Price had declined from its recent swing high back toward the 20-day Moving Average.

The decline was modest.

The candlesticks showed no obvious signs of weakness.

There was no significantly negative news.

But two details immediately caught my attention.

First, trading volume during this pullback was noticeably heavier than on previous pullbacks.

Second, after returning to the 20-day Moving Average, price failed to rebound as quickly as it had
in the past.

What did that tell us?

It told us that the market's response at this level had changed.

Although price had pulled back to the same moving average, previous pullbacks had attracted
aggressive buying.

This time, buyers were clearly hesitant, while sellers had become much more aggressive.

I turned to Ethan.

"What do you think a moving average actually is?"

"The average cost."

"Very good. Then what does the 20-day Moving Average represent?"

"The average cost over the past twenty trading days."

"If price remains above the 20-day Moving Average, what does that tell you?"

"It means the market's short-term average cost is below the current price, so most short-term
traders are still holding profitable positions."

"And if price pulls back to the 20-day Moving Average and then rebounds?"

"It means short-term participants are still willing to buy near their average cost."

"Then what if this time price returns to the 20-day Moving Average, trading volume expands, yet
price cannot bounce?"

Ethan paused.

He studied the chart before answering slowly.

"It means buying support around the average cost may be weakening."

"Exactly."

That is the true meaning of a moving average.

A moving average is not magical support.

It is simply a thermometer measuring the market's average cost.



I. A Moving Average Is Not a Wall—It Is a Cost Zone

I asked Ethan to enlarge the chart of Atlas Mobility.

Over the previous three months, the 20-day Moving Average had resembled a gently rising track,
with price spending most of its time above it.

Every time price approached the moving average, it rebounded.

At first glance, it looked as though the moving average itself was supporting the price.

But in reality, it wasn't the moving average supporting price.

It was the willingness of market participants to buy within that average cost zone that caused
price to rebound.

Without buyers, a moving average supports nothing.

I wrote a sentence on the whiteboard:

        Moving Average = Average Cost, Not Magic Support

II. The Difference Between the 20-Day and 50-Day Moving Averages

Ethan was already quite familiar with the 20-day Moving Average.

His understanding of the 50-day Moving Average, however, was still incomplete.

I asked him to add both the 20-day and 50-day Moving Averages to the chart of Atlas Mobility.

The picture immediately became much clearer.

The 20-day Moving Average tracked price much more closely and reacted more quickly.

The 50-day Moving Average was smoother and responded more slowly.

The 20-day Moving Average reflects the temperature of short-term market sentiment.

The 50-day Moving Average serves as the cost track of the intermediate-term trend.

During a strong uptrend, price normally trades above the 20-day Moving Average, while the 20-
day Moving Average itself remains above the 50-day Moving Average.

This tells us that the market's short-term average cost is higher than its intermediate-term average
cost, meaning investors are continually accepting higher price levels.

If price falls below the 20-day Moving Average but continues to hold above the 50-day Moving
Average, it may simply represent a short-term pullback.

However, if price breaks below both the 20-day and 50-day Moving Averages, and the moving
averages themselves begin to flatten or slope downward, investors should become alert to the
possibility that the nature of the trend is changing.

Price reached the 20-day Moving Average.

Ethan did not buy.

For him, that alone represented meaningful progress.

Only a few weeks earlier, he would have immediately concluded that this was another buying

bounced."

Now, however, he understood that he first needed to observe the market's reaction.

At eleven o'clock, price broke below the 20-day Moving Average.

Trading volume expanded noticeably.

Ethan frowned.

"This time feels different."

"In previous pullbacks, trading volume contracted, which suggested limited selling pressure. This
time, volume expanded as price broke below the moving average, meaning sellers have become
much more aggressive."

"Go on."

"And after breaking below, price didn't immediately recover. Instead, it stayed below the moving

IV. Moving Average Alignment: Measuring the Temperature of the Trend

The following day, Atlas Mobility continued to decline.

Price approached the 50-day Moving Average.

Discussion throughout the research department became much more intense.

One colleague believed it was nothing more than a normal correction within a growth stock.

Another trader argued that the short-term trend had already been damaged and that exposure
should be reduced.

This time, Ethan did not rush to take sides.

Instead, he switched the chart to the daily timeframe and marked the 20-day Moving Average, the
50-day Moving Average, and the recent swing highs and lows.

"At the moment," he said, "price has broken below the 20-day Moving Average, but the 50-day
Moving Average is still rising. If price can stabilize near the 50-day Moving Average on
declining volume and then reclaim the 20-day Moving Average, this may simply be a deeper
pullback within an intermediate-term uptrend."

I nodded.

"And if it can't?"

"If price breaks below the 50-day Moving Average on expanding volume, the 20-day Moving
Average begins turning downward, and price later fails to reclaim the 20-day Moving Average
during a rebound, then the trend is probably shifting from strength to weakness."

"Very good."

The alignment of moving averages helps us evaluate the temperature of a trend.

When short-term moving averages remain above long-term moving averages, and both continue
sloping upward, the trend is running at a relatively high temperature.

When the moving averages begin converging, momentum is fading, and the market is entering a
period of hesitation.

When the short-term moving average falls below the long-term moving average and both begin
sloping downward, the trend is likely weakening.

However, moving-average crossovers should never be applied mechanically.

By the time a genuine Death Cross appears, price has often already declined substantially.

Likewise, by the time a Golden Cross forms, price may already have advanced significantly.

Moving-average signals are inherently lagging indicators.

Therefore, moving-average crossovers should never serve as the sole basis for buying or selling.

Instead, they should be used as supporting confirmation of the market's trend condition.

Professor's Whiteboard Session

20-Day and 50-Day Moving Average Model

That evening, the topic of Ethan's whiteboard lesson was:

The 20-Day and 50-Day Moving Average Model.

Moving averages are among the most widely used tools in technical analysis.

Ironically, because they are so common, they are also among the most frequently misused.

Many investors open a chart and casually add the 5-day, 10-day, 20-day, 50-day, 100-day, and
200-day Moving Averages. The screen ends up covered with colorful lines that look professional,
yet when it comes time to trade, they become even more confused.

I said to Ethan,

        "More indicators do not make you a better trader. You must understand
        exactly what question each line is answering."



I. What Question Does the 20-Day Moving Average Answer?

The 20-day Moving Average generally represents the market's short- to intermediate-term
average cost.

It roughly corresponds to one trading month.

When price consistently trades above the 20-day Moving Average, it indicates that the short-term
trend remains relatively strong.

When price repeatedly pulls back to the 20-day Moving Average and rebounds, it suggests that
short-term market participants are still willing to buy near their average cost.

When price breaks below the 20-day Moving Average and cannot quickly recover, it indicates
that the short-term trend may be weakening.

However, because the 20-day Moving Average reacts quickly, it is also more susceptible to short-
term market fluctuations.


                                               - 176 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 185 / 686>
For that reason, it is well suited for observing market rhythm, but it should not be used by itself to
determine the long-term trend.



II. What Question Does the 50-Day Moving Average Answer?

The 50-day Moving Average is smoother and represents a broader intermediate-term market cost.

Because it does not follow price as closely as the 20-day Moving Average, it filters out much of
the market's short-term noise.

When price remains above the 50-day Moving Average and the moving average itself continues
rising, it usually indicates that the intermediate-term trend remains healthy.

When price falls below the 20-day Moving Average but continues to hold above the 50-day
Moving Average, it may simply represent a deeper correction.

However, when price breaks below the 50-day Moving Average on expanding trading volume
and then fails to reclaim it during a rebound, investors should become alert to the possibility that
the intermediate-term trend is changing.

The 50-day Moving Average is not an absolute line of defense.

But it is an important reference point for judging whether the trend remains healthy.



III. Four Moving Average Conditions

I drew four different moving-average conditions on the whiteboard.

First, price remains above both the 20-day and the 50-day Moving Averages.

This represents a strong trending environment.

If the 20-day Moving Average remains above the 50-day Moving Average, and both continue
sloping upward, it indicates that both short-term and intermediate-term average costs are rising,
reflecting a healthy market trend.

Second, price falls below the 20-day Moving Average but remains above the 50-day Moving
Average.

This represents a normal short-term correction.

The key question is whether buying support appears near the 50-day Moving Average.

Third, price breaks below the 50-day Moving Average.

This represents an intermediate-term risk signal.

If the breakdown is accompanied by expanding trading volume and a failed rebound, the trend
may be deteriorating significantly.

Fourth, the 20-day Moving Average falls below the 50-day Moving Average.

This is a lagging confirmation that the trend has weakened.

It should not be used as the sole sell signal, but it can serve as an important reference for position
management and risk control.

IV. Common Mistakes When Trading with Moving Averages

Mistake 1: Buying Simply Because Price Touches a Moving Average

Whether a moving average presents a buying opportunity depends entirely on Price Action.

If price pulls back on declining trading volume and then rebounds, it may represent an
opportunity.

If price breaks below the moving average on expanding trading volume and then fails to reclaim
it, it may instead signal increasing risk.



Mistake 2: Selling Immediately After Price Breaks Below a Moving Average

A short-term break below a moving average is sometimes nothing more than market noise.

The important questions are:

How significant is the breakdown?

Did trading volume expand?

Where did price close?

Can price quickly recover above the moving average?



Mistake 3: Believing That More Moving Averages Make You More Professional

Using too many moving averages often creates confusion rather than clarity.

For most trend analysis, the 20-day and 50-day Moving Averages provide more than enough
information to build a solid analytical framework.



Mistake 4: Ignoring the Overall Market Environment

During a bull market, the 20-day Moving Average often serves as an effective rhythm line for the
trend.

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 188 / 686>
During a bear market, however, price may repeatedly rebound toward the 20-day Moving
Average before continuing lower.

Moving averages must always be interpreted within the context of the broader market
environment.



V. How Do Moving Averages Work Together with Chart Patterns?

The greatest value of moving averages does not come from using them in isolation.

Their true value comes from combining them with Chart Patterns.

For example:

       When a Double Bottom breaks above its Neckline while simultaneously reclaiming both
        the 20-day and 50-day Moving Averages, it indicates that the market's trend condition is
        improving.
       During a V-Bottom Reversal, reclaiming the 20-day Moving Average may provide the
        first buying opportunity.
       During a Bull Flag, if price consistently holds above the 50-day Moving Average, it
        suggests that the intermediate-term trend remains healthy.
       After a Triple Top forms, if price breaks below both the 20-day and 50-day Moving
        Averages, the risk of a major top becomes further confirmed.

Moving averages are not the main characters.

They are tools that help us evaluate the temperature of a trend.

Case Review

Why Did the Moving Average Support Fail in Atlas Mobility?

Now let's return to Atlas Mobility.

On the surface, it looked like nothing more than another pullback to the 20-day Moving Average.

But the details told a very different story.

During the previous pullbacks to the 20-day Moving Average:

       Trading Volume contracted.
       Price rebounded quickly.
       The 20-day Moving Average continued sloping upward.
       Price never broke meaningfully below the moving average.




                                               - 181 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 190 / 686>
All of these signs indicated that buyers were still willing to accumulate shares near the market's
short-term average cost.

This time, however, the situation was different.

       Price broke below the 20-day Moving Average.
       Trading Volume expanded significantly.
       Price failed to recover quickly after the breakdown.
       The rebound attempt failed to reclaim the 20-day Moving Average.
       Price then continued drifting toward the 50-day Moving Average.

These changes suggested that the short-term trend had already begun to weaken.

The correct conclusion was not:

        "The 20-day Moving Average worked before, so it must work again this
        time."

Instead, the correct question was:

        "How is price behaving this time after reaching the 20-day Moving
        Average, and how does that behavior differ from previous pullbacks?"

That is the essence of professional trade review.

The market does not guarantee that a moving average will continue to work simply because it has
worked in the past.

When market behavior changes, the significance of the moving average changes as well.



Chapter Trading Rules

That day, I asked Ethan to write down five trading rules.

First, a moving average represents average cost, not magical support.

Second, the 20-day Moving Average is best suited for observing the rhythm of the short-term
trend, while the 50-day Moving Average is better suited for evaluating the health of the
intermediate-term trend.

Third, whether a pullback to a moving average is healthy depends on Trading Volume and Price
Action.

Fourth, when price breaks below a moving average on expanding trading volume and then fails to
reclaim it, support may have turned into resistance.

Chapter Summary

In this chapter, Ethan Carter learned how to understand moving averages correctly.

He no longer viewed them as automatic buy-and-sell signals.

Instead, he came to understand that:

       The 20-day Moving Average represents the market's short-term average cost.
       The 50-day Moving Average represents a more stable intermediate-term trend cost.
       Trading above a moving average does not mean a stock is permanently safe.
       Breaking below a moving average does not automatically mean the trend has reversed.
       What matters most is how price behaves around the moving average.

If price pulls back to the moving average on declining Trading Volume and then rebounds
quickly, buyers are still willing to accumulate shares near the market's average cost.

If price breaks below the moving average on expanding Trading Volume and then fails to reclaim
it during a rebound, the original cost support has likely begun to fail.

From this chapter onward, Ethan no longer asked:

        "Can I buy simply because price has reached the moving average?"

Instead, he began asking:

        "Is the market still willing to accumulate shares near this average cost?"

        "Was Trading Volume abnormal during the breakdown?"
