Chapter 25
Risk-Reward Ratio: Not Every Opportunity Is Worth Trading


After Ethan Carter mastered Position Sizing, the mistakes he made became more sophisticated.

In the beginning, he bought because of emotion.

Later, he bought because of chart patterns.

Then he bought because of confirmed Breakouts.

Now he knew how to write a trading plan, calculate a Stop-Loss, and control his position size.

But the market always presents the next challenge the moment you master the previous one.

This time, the lesson was simple:

A trade that looks correct is not necessarily a trade worth taking.

Many investors spend their entire lives without truly understanding that sentence.

They ask only:

        "Will this stock go up?"

        "Is this sector strong?"

        "Is this chart pattern valid?"

        "Is this breakout genuine?"

Those questions are certainly important.

But they are not enough.

Professional traders must also ask:

        "If I'm wrong, how much will I lose?"

        "If I'm right, how much can I make?"

        "Is the potential reward worth the risk I'm taking?"

That is the essence of the Risk-Reward Ratio.

Some opportunities in the market look excellent—but they arrive too late.

The price has already risen substantially.

The Stop-Loss is far away.

The next major Resistance level is close.

You might still make a small profit if you buy.

But if the trade fails, the downside is even greater.

Such trades are not impossible to profit from.

They are simply poor bargains.

Ethan did not truly understand this until he studied a European energy storage company.

For the purposes of this book, I will call it Solara Storage Systems.

II. Going Up Does Not Necessarily Mean It Is Worth Buying

Many investors find this idea extremely difficult to accept.

They often say:

        "But it really did go higher afterward."

        "My market direction was correct."

        "If I had bought it, I still would have made money."

All of those statements may be true.

But trading is not about proving afterward that your analysis was correct.

Trading is about deciding before placing the order whether the risk is worth taking.

If a trade offers only a 5% potential gain while exposing you to a 5% loss, you need a very high
win rate to remain profitable over the long run.

If another trade offers a 10% potential gain with only 3% downside risk, even a moderate win rate
can produce a profitable trading system.

That is why professional traders do not ask only,

        "Will it go up?"

They also ask:

        "How much drawdown must I accept before the stock reaches my target?"

        "If the trade fails, how much will I lose?"

        "Is the potential reward sufficient to justify the risk?"

IV. A Measured Move Is Not a Crystal Ball

Ethan asked,

        "How do we determine the target price? If I simply set a higher target,
        wouldn't the Risk-Reward Ratio improve?"

That is one of the most common mistakes investors make.

Many traders artificially raise their target prices simply to make a trade appear more attractive.

Suppose the reasonable target is €51.

If the Risk-Reward Ratio does not look attractive enough, they suddenly claim the target should
be €55.

If that still isn't enough, they stretch it to €60 and call it a long-term objective.

Doing so serves no purpose.

A target price is not meant to make a trade look better.

It must be based on objective analysis.

There are three common methods for establishing a price target.

First: A previous Resistance zone.

Second: A Measured Move derived from the chart pattern.

Third: Ongoing trend management within a continuing trend.

Why was €50–€51 the appropriate target for Solara Storage Systems?

Because the stock had previously encountered a significant area of heavy trading activity there,
and the measured move from its most recent consolidation projected into roughly the same price
zone.

Without another breakout or a fresh consolidation, simply increasing the target to €60 in order to
improve the Risk-Reward Ratio is not analysis.

It is wishful thinking.

I told Ethan,

I. What Is the Risk-Reward Ratio?

The Risk-Reward Ratio measures the relationship between a trade's potential profit and its
potential loss.

The formula is straightforward:

Risk-Reward Ratio = Potential Reward ÷ Potential Risk

Potential Risk = Entry Price − Stop-Loss

Potential Reward = Target Price − Entry Price

For example:



                                               - 570 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 579 / 686>
Entry Price: €26.00

Stop-Loss: €24.50

Target Price: €30.50

Potential Risk: €1.50

Potential Reward: €4.50

The Risk-Reward Ratio is therefore:

4.50 ÷ 1.50 = 3

Or expressed another way:

1:3

This means you are willing to risk one unit in pursuit of three units of potential reward.

II. Why Is the Risk-Reward Ratio So Important?

Because you will never win every trade.

Every trading system experiences losses.

The purpose of the Risk-Reward Ratio is to allow you to maintain a long-term edge without
requiring an extremely high win rate.

Assume every trade risks one unit.

If your average Risk-Reward Ratio is 1 : 3, then every winning trade earns three units, while
every losing trade costs only one unit.

Even if you win only 40% of your trades, you may still achieve positive long-term results.

However, if your Risk-Reward Ratio is only 1 : 1, you need a much higher win rate to remain
consistently profitable.

If your Risk-Reward Ratio falls below 1 : 1, maintaining profitability may require an
exceptionally high win rate, placing tremendous pressure on your trading system.

That is why trading is not determined solely by your win rate.

It also depends on your payoff ratio.

Your win rate tells you how often you are right.

Your payoff ratio tells you how much you earn when you are right—and how much you lose
when you are wrong.

Long-term performance is the product of both.

III. Where Do Measured Move Targets Come From?

A Measured Move is a method of estimating a stock's potential price objective based on its chart
structure.

Common methods include:

1. Trading Range Measured Move

Measure the height of the trading range:

Height = Resistance − Support

After a confirmed Breakout, project that height upward from the breakout level.

For example:

Support: €22


                                             - 573 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 582 / 686>
Resistance: €26

Height: €4

Projected upside target after the breakout:

Approximately €30

2. Flag Pattern Measured Move

For a Bull Flag, project the height of the flagpole upward.

For a Bear Flag, project the height of the flagpole downward.

3. Double Bottom or Inverse Head and Shoulders

Measure the distance from the lowest point to the Neckline, then project that distance upward
from the breakout.

4. Double Top or Triple Top

Measure the distance from the top of the pattern to the Neckline, then project that distance
downward after the breakdown.

A Measured Move is not a prediction.

It is simply a practical method for estimating potential price movement and evaluating whether
the resulting Risk-Reward Ratio is attractive.

IV. The Minimum Standard for a Good Risk-Reward Ratio

Different trading strategies require different Risk-Reward Ratios.

However, for most swing trading strategies, I expected Ethan to pursue at least:

1:2

In other words, the potential reward should be at least twice the potential risk.

Even better opportunities should approach:

1:3

At the same time, I reminded him of something equally important.

Some high-probability strategies can justify a lower Risk-Reward Ratio.

Some lower-probability breakout strategies require a much higher one.

When market conditions deteriorate, your standards should become more demanding.

When market conditions are exceptionally strong, you may allow yourself slightly more
flexibility.

But regardless of the environment, trades with a Risk-Reward Ratio below 1 : 1 are generally not

V. The Risk-Reward Ratio Changes as Price Changes

The Risk-Reward Ratio is never fixed.

The very same stock can offer completely different Risk-Reward Ratios at different prices.

Buying near Support usually provides a nearby Stop-Loss and a more distant target, creating an
attractive Risk-Reward Ratio.

Buying after a confirmed Breakout may still offer a reasonable balance.

Chasing an extended move, however, often causes the Risk-Reward Ratio to deteriorate rapidly.

That is why every meaningful price change requires you to recalculate the trade.

A stock that represented an excellent opportunity yesterday is not automatically an excellent
opportunity today.

When it first broke above its small consolidation and successfully completed its Retest, the Risk-
Reward Ratio may have been highly attractive.

But by the time Ethan noticed it, the price had already approached its measured move target.
