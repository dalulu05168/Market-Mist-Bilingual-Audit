The Chessboard of Position Sizing: Why Two Traders Can Be Right Yet End Up
With Completely Different Results
After Ethan Carter learned how to use Stop-Losses, I thought he would become a more cautious
trader.

For a while, he did.

But the market soon presented him with another challenge.

This time, it was not testing whether he could accept a stop-loss.

It was testing whether he had the discipline to control his position size.

Many investors believe that as long as they correctly predict market direction, everything else
will take care of itself.

They often say:

        "I was right about that stock."

        "That sector really did rally later."

        "I knew it was going to break out."

But the market does not reward you simply because your directional analysis is correct.

Two traders can identify exactly the same opportunity.

One earns consistent profits.

Another makes only a small gain.

A third still loses money—or even blows up the account.

What makes the difference?

Position Size.

Position sizing is one of the most underestimated aspects of trading.

Because it is not as visually obvious as a chart.

It is not as logical as financial statements.

It is not as exciting as breaking news.

And it is certainly not as emotionally satisfying as clicking the Buy button.

Your market analysis tells you what might happen.

At first, they trade without a plan.

Increase it by how much?

Based on what criteria?

What is the maximum acceptable loss?

If the trade fails, can your account comfortably absorb the loss?

III. Position Size Is Not Determined by How Much You Want to Buy—It Is Determined by How
Much You Can Afford to Lose

When calculating position size, many investors ask:

        "How much should I buy?"

That is an incomplete question.

A more professional question is:

        "If I'm wrong, what is the maximum amount I am willing to lose?"

Position size is not determined by how much you hope to make.

It is determined by how much you are willing to lose.

I wrote a simple formula on the whiteboard:



                                                - 537 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 546 / 686>
        Position Size = Account Risk ÷ Risk per Share

Suppose the account size is €1,000,000.

If Ethan limits the maximum risk on any single trade to 1%, then the maximum acceptable loss is
€10,000.

AsterWorks has a planned entry price of €76.

Its Stop-Loss is €72.

That means the Risk per Share is €4.

The maximum position size would therefore be:

€10,000 ÷ €4 = 2,500 shares

The capital required would be:

2,500 × €76 = €190,000

Or approximately 19% of the account.

Notice what this means.

It does not mean Ethan must buy a 19% position.

It simply means that, given a 1% account risk and a €4 Stop-Loss, 19% represents the theoretical
maximum position size.

If portfolio correlation is taken into account, he may decide to risk only 0.6% of the account.

That reduces the maximum acceptable loss to €6,000.

The calculation then becomes:

€6,000 ÷ €4 = 1,500 shares

The required capital would be approximately €114,000, or roughly 11.4% of the account.

That is how position sizing is calculated.

Not by intuition.

Not by courage.

Not by saying,

        "This is a great opportunity."

But by allocating a predefined risk budget.

Adding to an existing position is often more dangerous than making the initial purchase.

Because by then, the trade is already showing a profit.

People begin telling themselves:

        "I already have a profit cushion."

        "The trend has already proven itself."

        "Buying a little more won't hurt."

        "This time I can make a much bigger profit."

But every additional purchase must still be governed by a risk management plan.

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 551 / 686>
I told Ethan that there are three rules for adding to a position.

First, only add after the trade has developed as expected.

Never add to a losing position.

The only exception is when your original trading plan specifically calls for scaling into the
position, with each entry carrying its own clearly defined risk.

Second, every additional purchase must be based on a new confirmation signal.

For example:

       A successful retest after the breakout.
       A second breakout from a Bull Flag.
       A pullback on lighter Trading Volume after reaching a new high.

Never add simply because the stock has gone higher.

Third, your total portfolio risk must remain under control after adding to the position.

If price falls back below a key level after you add shares, you must already know:

How much will the entire position lose?

Does the total risk still remain within your account's risk limits?

I. What Determines Pattern Quality?

Pattern quality is not simply whether a chart "looks good."




                                               - 545 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 554 / 686>
It is determined by multiple factors.

First: Market Environment

Does the overall market support the trade?

Is market risk appetite stable?

Is the major index in an uptrend, moving sideways, or in a downtrend?

Second: Sector Strength

Is the stock's sector demonstrating Relative Strength?

Is it benefiting from sector rotation?

Is it currently one of the market's leadership groups?

Third: Pattern Duration

Has the pattern had sufficient time to develop?

Patterns that form too quickly are often less reliable.

The longer the consolidation, the more complete the transfer of ownership tends to be.

Fourth: Trading Volume

Has Trading Volume contracted before the breakout?

Does volume expand during the breakout?

Does volume contract again during the retest?

Fifth: Clarity of Key Price Levels

Are Support, Resistance, the Neckline, or the boundaries of the Bull Flag clearly defined?

The clearer these levels are, the easier it becomes to build a precise trading plan.

Sixth: Stop-Loss Distance

Is the stop-loss placed at a logical distance?

If it is too wide, position size must be reduced.

If it is too tight, normal market fluctuations may trigger it prematurely.

Seventh: Relative Strength

Is the stock outperforming both the overall market and its industry peers?




                                                 - 546 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 555 / 686>
Leading stocks are more likely to continue attracting institutional capital.

Eighth: Portfolio Correlation

Do you already own similar positions?

If you do, any additional exposure should be discounted accordingly.

II. How Should Position Sizes Be Classified?

I suggested that Ethan divide position sizes into four categories.

Exploratory Position

Used for potential setups that have not yet been fully confirmed.

The risk is kept low, and the position remains small.

Its primary purpose is to monitor the opportunity while maintaining market exposure.

Standard Position

Used for trades with clear confirmation signals, high-quality chart patterns, and supportive market
conditions.

This should be the position size you use most frequently.

High-Conviction Position

Reserved for opportunities where multiple pieces of evidence align:

       An outstanding chart pattern.
       A clearly defined Stop-Loss.
       Strong technical confirmation.
       Low portfolio correlation.

Even then, position size should never be increased without limits.

No Position

Used when the chart pattern is unclear, confirmation is insufficient, the Risk-Reward Ratio is
unattractive, the stop-loss cannot be clearly defined, or overall portfolio risk is already too high.

Sometimes, the most professional position size is zero.

III. Fixed Allocation vs. Risk-Based Position Sizing

A fixed allocation means investing the same percentage of capital in every trade.

For example, buying 10% of your account in every position.

The method is simple.

But it has a significant weakness.

Different trades require different Stop-Loss distances.

Suppose one stock is purchased at 100, with a stop-loss at 95.

The Risk per Share is 5%.

Another stock is also purchased at 100, but its stop-loss is 85.



                                               - 549 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 558 / 686>
The Risk per Share is 15%.

If both positions receive the same 10% allocation, the second trade actually carries three times the
risk of the first.

A risk-based position sizing approach works differently.

It first determines how much of the account you are willing to lose.

Only then does it calculate the appropriate number of shares based on the Stop-Loss distance.

This is a far more professional approach.

Because it ensures that every trade has a consistent impact on the overall account.

V. The Ultimate Purpose of Position Sizing

The purpose of position sizing is not to eliminate losses.

Losses are unavoidable.

The purpose of position sizing is to ensure that no single losing trade can destroy your account.

At the same time, it allows you to commit meaningful capital when genuinely high-quality
opportunities appear.

It solves two fundamental problems.

First, when you are wrong, your losses remain controlled.

Second, when you are right, your profits become meaningful.

If your position is too small, even a correct analysis produces insignificant returns.

If your position is too large, one mistake can inflict severe damage.

Professional position sizing is about finding the balance between those two extremes.
