Chapter 1
Day One in the Research Department: Price Is Not the Answer—It Is the Question
The first time I met Ethan Carter was on a rainy Monday morning.

London rain has a character of its own. It is neither as violent as a storm nor as brief as a summer
shower in Southern Europe. Instead, it is fine, quiet, and persistent, seeping silently into people's
coats, leather shoes, and moods—much like market sentiment.

That morning, I arrived twenty minutes earlier than usual.

The Research Department had not fully awakened yet. The trading screens were already on,
displaying the overnight performance of the U.S. market on the left while the closing data from
Asian markets continued to update. The coffee machine hummed softly. Several overnight traders
leaned back in their chairs, their eyes still carrying the fatigue of watching the Federal Reserve's
speech until three o'clock in the morning.

Our office occupied the twenty-first floor of a glass tower in London's financial district.

From the conference room, you could see a stretch of the River Thames. On sunny days, it
resembled a silver-gray ribbon. On gloomy days, it looked more like an emotionless balance sheet,
quietly recording the city's daily greed, fear, losses, and hope.

At 8:09 a.m., Ethan Carter walked in.

He looked younger than I had expected.

He wore a navy-blue suit, a white dress shirt, and a carefully tied necktie. His leather shoes were
polished almost excessively—so clean that anyone could tell it was his first day at a research
institution. He carried a laptop under his left arm and an Americano in his right hand. The lid of
the coffee cup was not fully secured, leaving a small trace of brown coffee along its rim.

As he entered the Research Department, he glanced at the reception desk before quickly scanning
the entire open-plan office.

It was the unmistakable look of a newcomer.

He wanted to appear composed, yet could not stop his eyes from being drawn to the price screens.
He hoped others would see him as well prepared, while secretly worrying they might discover
how unfamiliar everything still was.

I sat at the far end of the conference room and did not call him over immediately.

When someone walks into the market for the first time, you can often read a great deal from their
eyes.

In Ethan's eyes, I saw confidence, excitement, nervousness, and one of the most common
illusions shared by young research analysts:

He believed that if he worked hard enough, he would soon understand the market.

That was not necessarily a bad thing.

Without confidence, few people can survive in the investment industry. Every day, the market
confronts you with uncertainty. If someone is unwilling to make even the most basic judgment,
they will eventually be overwhelmed by volatility.

Excessive confidence, however, is an entirely different danger.

The market excels at one thing above all else:

It shatters confidence into pieces until you can no longer tell whether you are defending your
logic or merely protecting your ego.

At that moment, Ethan knew none of this.

He had no idea that on his very first day in the Research Department, he was about to receive an
expensive lesson from what appeared to be a beautiful candlestick.

I. The First Stock at the Morning Meeting

The morning meeting began at 8:30 a.m.

Research department morning meetings were usually brief, but the pace was always fast.
Everyone understood that the time before the market opened was extremely valuable. The macro
team began with an overview of the overnight U.S. market, the U.S. Dollar Index, the 10-year
Treasury yield, and major commodity prices. The industry team then updated the key sectors in
the European market. The quantitative team reported capital flows, volatility, and unusual trading
activity. Finally, the equity research analysts presented the stocks that deserved close attention
during the day.

Ethan sat in the third seat on the right side of the conference table.

That was the position where newcomers were most easily observed.

It was close enough to the large display to clearly see the charts, and close enough to me for me
to observe every change in his expression.

Seventeen minutes into the meeting, a European technology stock appeared on the screen.

To keep readers focused on the underlying principles rather than the actual company, I will refer
to it simply as NovaTech.

NovaTech was a cloud computing software company. It had experienced rapid growth over the
past several years, and its valuation had never been cheap. Its share price had gone through a

The price had risen.

Ethan paused.

        "Because the price failed to move higher every time it reached this level."

        "That describes what happened," I replied. "I'm asking why it happened."

The room became quiet once again.

I did not press Ethan any further. Instead, I turned to the others.

        "Why does the price repeatedly decline from the same area?"

A senior trader answered,

        "There may be trapped holders. Investors who bought near the previous
        highs want to sell as soon as the price returns close to their cost basis."

A portfolio manager added,

        "It could also be an institutional distribution zone. Instead of continuing to
        bid prices higher, institutions may be using that area to distribute shares to
        momentum buyers."

A colleague from the quantitative team said,

        "Based on the trading data over the past few weeks, transaction density in
        that area has been extremely high. It is clearly a major supply zone."

I looked back at Ethan.

        "Do you hear that? A resistance level is not some mysterious line. The
        price doesn't stop there simply because we draw a line on a chart. It
        represents the collective memory of market participants—a zone created
        by cost basis, expectations, fear, and concentrated selling pressure."

Ethan nodded.

But I could tell he had not truly absorbed the lesson.

His eyes were still fixed on the pre-market candlestick climbing higher.

Young investors share one common characteristic:

When prices are rising, it is very difficult for them to pay attention to risk.

II. A Breakout Is Not the Answer

I stood up and walked to the whiteboard.

        "Volume," he answered without hesitation.

        "And what does volume represent?"

        "It shows that money is participating."

        "More precisely," I said, "volume tells us whether the market is willing to

        "What do you mean by 'accepted by the market'?"

        "A price briefly trading above a certain level does not mean the market has
        accepted that new price," I explained. "Only when the price can remain
        above a key level after the breakout, when selling pressure weakens during
        a pullback, and when the volume structure remains healthy, can we say the
        market is accepting this new trading range."

Excitement, however, reduces every question to a single action:

III. The Market Gives the Lesson

At 9:00 a.m., the European market officially opened.

NovaTech opened 10.2% higher.

A few quiet remarks could be heard around the conference room.

Ethan's expression became complicated.

He said nothing, but I knew exactly what he was thinking.

If I had bought earlier, I would already be making money.

It is an agonizing feeling.

For young research analysts in particular, missing a profitable opportunity can sometimes feel
even worse than taking a loss. A loss at least means you participated. Missing the move makes
you feel that you lacked conviction.

The market often exploits this psychology to push investors into making mistakes.

Fifteen minutes after the opening bell, NovaTech continued climbing.

Its maximum gain approached 13%.

The higher the price rose, the quieter Ethan became.

Sometimes silence is not a sign of composure.

It simply means a person is arguing with himself.

At 10:20 a.m., the situation began to change.

NovaTech stopped advancing.

Several candlesticks with long upper shadows appeared on the intraday chart. Trading volume
remained extremely high, but every rally attempt was quickly pushed back down.

A senior trader sitting beside the trading desk quietly remarked,

        "There's supply up there."

The sentence was brief.

But it carried considerable weight.

What did "there's supply" mean?

It meant that someone was selling.

And not just retail investors selling a few shares.

When heavy trading repeatedly occurs within a price zone, yet the price fails to move higher, it
often indicates that significant selling pressure is being actively released.

Buyers are indeed buying.

But sellers are also taking advantage of abundant liquidity to distribute their shares.

Many investors see expanding volume and immediately assume that money is flowing into the
market.

What they forget is this:

Volume is always two-sided.

Every transaction has both a buyer and a seller.

The important question is not whether trading is taking place.

The important question is:

Who is being more aggressive?

Its gain narrowed to 7%.

At 11:15 a.m., the price returned to the morning breakout level.

Ethan finally spoke.

Hope is not analysis.

A sound judgment must be supported by evidence.

Hope requires nothing more than emotion.

What had appeared to be a strong breakout in the morning had, by the close, become a failed

Professor's Whiteboard Session

Chart Pattern Precedes Trend

That evening, I gave Ethan his first whiteboard lesson since joining the Research Department.

The topic was not a specific chart pattern.

Instead, it was the underlying principle shared by every technical pattern:

Patterns Are the Starting Point of Trends

In the financial markets, prices rarely enter a sustained trend without preparation.

A lasting trend almost always emerges from a pre-existing market structure.

That structure may take many forms:

A double bottom, a triple bottom, an inverse head and shoulders, a box breakout, a bull flag, a
bear flag, a double top, a triple top, or even a sharp reversal following a false breakout.

Regardless of the pattern, they all share the same underlying principle:




                                               - 15 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 24 / 686>
Buyers and sellers battle repeatedly within a certain price zone until one side gains a decisive
advantage and breaks the balance.

While the price remains inside the pattern, the market is in a state of equilibrium or stalemate.

Buyers believe the price represents value and are willing to accumulate shares.

Sellers believe the price is still too high, or that the prevailing trend remains weak, and are willing
to sell.

As a result, the price fluctuates repeatedly within a trading range.

These fluctuations are far from meaningless.

They represent an exchange of shares.

They also represent an exchange of conviction.

Some investors lose patience during consolidation.

Some are shaken out during pullbacks.

Some institutional investors quietly accumulate shares near the bottom.

Some short-term traders establish positions before the breakout.

Others rush in only after the breakout occurs.

When all of these actions are reflected on the chart, they create what we call a chart pattern.

II. What Is a Chart Pattern?

A chart pattern is not a drawing game.

Many people make the same mistake when they begin studying technical analysis:

They memorize patterns.

What does a double bottom look like?

What does a triple top look like?

What does an inverse head and shoulders look like?

What does a flag pattern look like?

They memorize pattern names the way students memorize vocabulary words.

But they forget to ask the far more important question:

Why did the price form this pattern in the first place?



                                                 - 18 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 27 / 686>
The essence of every chart pattern lies in the behavior of market participants.

A double bottom is important not because it resembles the letter W, but because the price tests the
same support area twice without breaking lower, suggesting that buying pressure is strengthening
while selling pressure is becoming exhausted.

A triple top is dangerous not because it has three peaks, but because repeated failures to break
above the same resistance indicate that sellers remain in firm control while buyers are gradually
losing strength.

A flag pattern signals trend continuation not because it resembles a flag, but because the low-
volume consolidation following a strong trend suggests that profit-taking is being absorbed while
the dominant trend participants remain committed.

Therefore, studying chart patterns is not about recognizing shapes.

It is about understanding changes in the balance of market forces.

III. How Does a Chart Pattern Become a Trend?

A complete trend reversal or trend continuation generally unfolds in four stages.

Stage One: The Existing Trend Begins to Slow

In a downtrend, the price no longer makes new lows with ease.

In an uptrend, the price no longer makes new highs effortlessly.

This indicates that the dominant force behind the existing trend is beginning to weaken.



Stage Two: A Market Structure Forms

The price starts fluctuating repeatedly within a particular price range.

It may be a bottoming formation.

It may be a topping formation.

It may develop into a trading range.

It may evolve into a flag pattern.

It may become a double bottom.

Or it may become a triple top.

This is the stage where buyers and sellers compete for control.

Stage Three: The Breakout of a Key Level



                                                - 19 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 28 / 686>
In a bottoming pattern, the price breaks above the neckline or a major resistance level.

In a topping pattern, the price breaks below the neckline or a significant support level.

In a continuation pattern, the price breaks out of the consolidation range.

This is the moment when market equilibrium is finally broken.



Stage Four: Confirmation and Trend Continuation

What happens after the breakout is often more important than the breakout itself.

If the price breaks above resistance and later retests that level without falling back below it, the
former resistance may become new support.

If the price breaks below support and subsequently rallies but fails to reclaim that level, the
former support may become new resistance.

A genuine trend usually begins after confirmation, not at the moment of the breakout itself.

IV. Why Is Volume So Important?

Price tells us where the market is moving.

Volume tells us whether the move has real conviction behind it.

If the price breaks above resistance but trading volume does not expand meaningfully, we should
question whether buying interest is genuinely strong enough to sustain the breakout.

If the price breaks below a critical support level while trading volume expands sharply, it often
indicates that selling pressure is accelerating.

If volume contracts during a pullback following a breakout, it suggests that selling pressure is
weakening and the underlying market structure remains healthy.

If the price continues to rise near market highs while volume gradually declines, investors should
be alert to the possibility that buying momentum is fading.

That is why I told Ethan,

        "Volume is not a secondary indicator.

        Volume is the witness to price action."

Price can be deceptive.

Although volume can occasionally be manipulated, it is far more difficult to disguise over an
extended period.

Especially around key price levels, changes in volume often reveal answers to questions such as:

Who is accumulating positions?

Who is exiting positions?

Is the breakout genuine?

Is the decline driven by panic selling?

Is the rebound supported by real buying interest?
