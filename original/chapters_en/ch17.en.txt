Chapter 17
The Bear Market Elevator: Why Declines Always Move Faster Than Advances


A bull market is like climbing a staircase.

It moves upward one step at a time.

Advance. Pullback. Advance again. Another pullback.

Investors have time to doubt, time to regret, and time to gradually convince themselves to join the
trend.

A bear market is completely different.

A bear market is like an elevator.

The doors close. The lights flicker. And suddenly, prices begin falling.

Before you fully understand what has happened, your portfolio has already dropped another floor.

Just as you begin telling yourself, "It's only a pullback," the elevator plunges again.

The first time Ethan Carter truly experienced the speed of a bear market was through a banking
ETF.

I will refer to it as the Euro Regional Banks ETF.

The fund tracked European regional banks, commercial banks, and several financial services
companies.

The previous year, banking stocks had performed exceptionally well as interest rates rose.

The market believed that higher interest rates would improve net interest margins and strengthen
bank profitability.

During that period, bank stocks were widely favored.

The reasons appeared convincing:

Higher interest rates.

Reasonable valuations.

Healthy dividend yields.

Improving balance sheets.

Upward earnings revisions.

But financial stocks have one unique characteristic.

When they appear to be at their safest, they often conceal the market's most sensitive risks.

Banks are not ordinary companies.

They do not sell shoes, coffee, or software subscriptions.

They trade in credit.

And once confidence in credit begins to weaken, prices can react with remarkable speed.

That spring, the market began worrying about a slowing European economy, the quality of
commercial real estate loans, and growing balance sheet pressure at several small and mid-sized
banks.

At first, those concerns appeared only in a handful of research reports.

Few investors paid much attention.

The ETF itself experienced only a modest pullback.

Initially, Ethan believed it was simply a normal correction.

"The banking ETF is only down about 6% from its highs. It's still trading near the 50-day Moving
Average," he said. "If the economic data doesn't deteriorate significantly, this area could provide
Support."

I studied the chart without answering immediately.

The long-term Market Structure had not yet been completely damaged.

But in the short term, several warning signs had already appeared.

Each rebound was making a lower high.

Trading Volume expanded on down days.

The 20-day Moving Average had begun turning downward.

I. The First Sharp Decline: When Doubt Turns Into Fear

The real decline began on a Monday.

Over the weekend, a mid-sized bank issued an impairment warning, citing increasing pressure
within its commercial real estate loan portfolio.

The announcement itself was not disastrous. But it struck the market's most vulnerable nerve.

When trading opened on Monday, the Euro Regional Banks ETF gapped down 3%.

The moment Ethan arrived at the office, his screen was filled with red.

-3.5%.

-4.2%.

-5.8%.

Several constituent stocks were down more than 8%.

The atmosphere inside the trading room changed completely.

During a bull market, people discuss target prices, growth opportunities, and valuation premiums.

During a bear market, everyone asks only one question:

Who will be sold next?

Staring at the ETF chart, Ethan said, "It's falling too fast."

"That is the nature of credit-sensitive assets," I replied. "Confidence builds slowly. But doubt
spreads quickly."

By the close, the Euro Regional Banks ETF had broken below its 50-day Moving Average and
fallen through its previous Support level on heavy Trading Volume.

The daily chart formed a large bearish candlestick.

Trading Volume was more than twice its average level over the previous month.

Ethan said, "That support has failed."

"Correct."

"So should we wait for a rebound before reducing our position?"

"You may wait for a rebound," I replied, "but never mistake a rebound for a trend reversal."

That sentence is one of the most important survival principles in a bear market.

Bear markets do not decline every day.

They rally as well.

Some of those rallies can be surprisingly powerful.

Such headlines are common during a bear market.

After every sharp decline, someone claims the market has fallen too far.

After every rebound, someone insists the worst is over.

Whenever valuations become cheaper, someone declares that opportunity has arrived.

But in a bear market, a lower price does not mean the risk has disappeared.

Watching the rebound, Ethan asked, "Is this a buying opportunity?"

"What do you think has actually changed?"

"Price is still below both the 20-day and 50-day Moving Averages."

"Keep going."

"It hasn't broken above the previous rebound high."

"It's simply rebounding from an oversold condition. The downtrend itself hasn't changed."

I nodded.

"Exactly. This is a bear market rally."

Bear market rallies are particularly deceptive because they move quickly.

After a steep decline, short covering, bottom fishing, and technical rebounds can all push prices
higher.

But if the rebound lacks Trading Volume and cannot break through key Resistance, it is not a
trend reversal. It is merely a pause within an existing downtrend.

III. A Bear Flag Is Not a Reversal—It Is a Pause Before the Next Decline

I drew the Bear Flag on the whiteboard.

First, a steep downward move—the flagpole.

Then, a small upward-sloping channel—the flag.

Finally, a breakdown below the lower boundary of that channel.

"What is the essence of a Bear Flag?" I asked Ethan.

He replied, "After a sharp decline, the market experiences a brief rebound. But the rebound lacks
strength. Selling pressure hasn't disappeared. It's simply taking a short break. Once the rebound
fails, the downtrend resumes."

"Very good."

A Bear Flag is not important because it resembles a flag. It is important because it reflects typical
bear market behavior.

The initial sharp decline causes some short sellers to take profits, while attracting short-term
bargain hunters. As a result, price rebounds.

But if Trading Volume continues shrinking throughout that rebound, it tells us that fresh buying
demand is insufficient.

If the rebound cannot break above key Moving Averages or former Support levels, it tells us that
selling pressure still dominates overhead.

And if price breaks below the lower boundary of the flag, the temporary recovery has ended, and
sellers have regained control.

At that point, the downtrend is likely to continue.

I. The Components of a Bear Flag

A standard Bear Flag usually consists of two parts.

Part One: The Flagpole

This is a sharp, rapid decline.

Price breaks below Support. Trading Volume expands. Market sentiment deteriorates quickly.

Part Two: The Flag

After the sharp decline, price experiences a brief rebound or sideways consolidation.

This usually develops into a small upward-sloping channel.

The flag portion has several typical characteristics:

        The rebound is relatively slow.

        Trading Volume gradually declines.

        Price cannot break above major Resistance.

        Each rebound high remains below the previous significant high.

When price breaks below the lower boundary of the flag on expanding Trading Volume, the Bear
Flag is confirmed.

II. The Market Psychology Behind a Bear Flag

There are generally three groups of participants behind a Bear Flag.

The first group consists of short sellers who take profits after the initial sharp decline. Their
buying activity helps fuel the rebound.




                                                - 386 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 395 / 686>
The second group consists of bargain hunters. They believe the market has fallen too far and
begin buying in anticipation of a recovery.

The third group consists of trapped long investors. The rebound gives them hope. But if price
fails to continue higher, many of them sell during the next decline.

The flag portion is essentially a temporary balance between these competing forces.

However, if the rebound fails to attract sustained buying interest, price eventually breaks below
the flag, and the downtrend resumes.

III. The Difference Between a Bear Flag and a True Reversal

A genuine trend reversal usually displays several important characteristics.

Price stops making new lows.

Trading Volume increases during the rebound.

Price breaks above the previous rebound high.

Price regains its key Moving Averages.

The Retest holds successfully.

Market breadth begins improving.

A Bear Flag, on the other hand, shows the opposite characteristics.

A sharp decline comes first.

The rebound occurs on declining Trading Volume.

The rebound fails to break above key Resistance.

Price remains below important Moving Averages.

And once price breaks below the flag, selling pressure returns.

For that reason, you should never conclude that a trend has reversed simply because price has
rallied for several days.

A rebound is merely a phenomenon.

IV. How to Trade a Bear Flag

A Bear Flag can be used from two different perspectives.

For Long Positions

A Bear Flag rally is primarily a risk management opportunity.

If you are holding a declining asset, and price rebounds into a resistance zone without sufficient
Trading Volume, you should consider reducing your position instead of adding to it.




                                              - 388 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 397 / 686>
If price breaks below the lower boundary of the flag, your long position must be re-evaluated.

For Short Positions

After a confirmed Bear Flag Breakdown, there may be an opportunity to trade with the prevailing
downtrend.

However, short-selling carries greater risk and is not appropriate for every investor.

If you choose to trade from the short side, you must clearly define:

        Your entry price.

        Your stop-loss level.

        Your target area.

        Your position size.

        Whether you will exit if price moves back into the flag.

Technical patterns are not meant to encourage impulsive trading. They exist to help you
understand where risk is located.

V. The Measured Move of a Bear Flag

The theoretical downside target of a Bear Flag is usually estimated by projecting the height of the
flagpole downward from the breakdown point.

For example:

The banking ETF declines from 100 to 86.

The flagpole measures approximately 14 points.

A Bear Flag then develops.

If price breaks below the flag near 90, the theoretical downside target would be approximately:

90 − 14 = 76

This is not a precise prediction. It is simply a method for estimating the potential downside risk.

Case Review

Why Is the Euro Regional Banks ETF a Classic Bear Flag?

Now, let us return to the Euro Regional Banks ETF.

Why is it an ideal example of a Bear Flag?

Because it perfectly illustrates the typical rhythm of a bear market.

First, the original bullish thesis was based on higher interest rates improving bank profitability.

Second, the market began worrying about credit risk and mounting pressure from commercial real

Third, the ETF broke below both the 50-day Moving Average and a major Support level on
expanding Trading Volume.

Fourth, a technical rebound followed the sharp decline.

Fifth, during the rebound, Trading Volume steadily contracted while price formed a small
upward-sloping channel.

Sixth, the rebound failed to reclaim key Resistance levels and major Moving Averages.

Seventh, price broke below the lower boundary of the flag as Trading Volume expanded once

That is a textbook Bear Flag.

If investors mistake the rebound inside the flag for a genuine reversal, they are likely to buy in the
middle of an ongoing downtrend.

But investors who recognize the Bear Flag understand something different.

This rebound is far more likely to represent a temporary pause after an initial wave of selling,
rather than the beginning of a new uptrend.

Chapter Trading Rules

That day, I asked Ethan to write down seven trading rules.

First, a bear market does not decline every day. Powerful rallies can also occur within a
downtrend.

Second, after a sharp decline, a low-volume rebound should immediately raise the possibility of a
Bear Flag.

Third, the flagpole is created by a rapid decline, while the flag is formed by a weak rebound.

Fourth, if the rebound cannot break above key Resistance levels and major Moving Averages,
never assume that the trend has reversed.

Fifth, a Bear Flag is confirmed only after price breaks below the lower boundary of the flag on

Chapter Summary

In this chapter, Ethan Carter finally understood the speed of a bear market.

He realized that:

A decline does not require many reasons.

Fear spreads much faster than confidence is built.

After Support is broken, a rebound may be nothing more than a brief pause.

A low-volume rebound following a sharp decline often develops into a Bear Flag.

And once the Bear Flag breaks down, the existing downtrend is likely to continue.

From this chapter onward, Ethan no longer rushed to declare that the market had reached a
bottom simply because prices rebounded.

Instead, he began asking:

        Is the rebound supported by Trading Volume?

        Has it broken above the previous rebound high?

        Has price reclaimed the major Moving Averages?

        Is the rebound merely returning to former Support, now acting as Resistance?
