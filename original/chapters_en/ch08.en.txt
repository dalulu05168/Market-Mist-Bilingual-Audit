Chapter 8
Candlesticks Are Not Candles—They Are Records of the Battlefield


Ethan Carter used to be fascinated by candlestick patterns.

More precisely, he was fascinated by their names.

Before joining the research firm, he had read numerous technical analysis books and taken
several online courses. He could recite many terms that sounded highly professional:

Hammer Candle.

Engulfing Pattern.

Doji.

Dark Cloud Cover.

Morning Star.

Evening Star.

Long Upper Shadow.

Long Lower Shadow.

He had even compiled a "Candlestick Pattern Cheat Sheet" and printed it out, placing it beside his
desk.

Yet despite all of that, he continued to make poor trading decisions in real market conditions.

Why?

Because he treated candlesticks as pictures.

Whenever he saw a long lower shadow, he assumed it signaled a market bottom.

Whenever he saw a large bullish candle, he believed the bulls had taken control.

Whenever he saw a Doji, he expected an immediate trend reversal.

Whenever he saw a long upper shadow, he assumed the uptrend had come to an end.

It was like memorizing every word in a language without knowing how to read an entire sentence.

The name of a candlestick is not what matters.

What matters is where it appears, what happened beforehand, how trading volume behaved,
where the candle closed, and whether the next candlestick confirms the signal.

In other words, a candlestick is not simply a candle.

It is a record of the battle between buyers and sellers over a specific period of time.



I. A "Beautiful" Long Lower Shadow

One morning, Ethan brought me a chart.

It belonged to a European semiconductor equipment company, which I will refer to as
SiliconWorks Europe.

Over the previous month, the stock had declined nearly 16% from its recent high. The day before,
it continued falling during the session, but suddenly rebounded in the afternoon, leaving a long
lower shadow by the close.

Ethan placed the chart in front of me, his voice filled with anticipation.

"Professor, is this a Hammer Candle?"

I glanced at the chart.

It certainly looked like one.

A small real body.

A long lower shadow.

The close was near the day's high.

It had appeared after a prolonged decline.

Judging by its appearance alone, it could easily be called a Hammer Candle.

But instead of answering his question, I asked one of my own.

"Why are you so concerned about what it's called?"

Ethan paused.

"Because a Hammer Candle usually means there's support below and that a reversal may be
starting."

"Usually?" I asked.

He hesitated for a moment.

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 157 / 686>
"At the end of a downtrend, it may indicate a reversal."

"Then how do you know this is the end of the downtrend?"

He fell silent.

That was exactly the problem.

Many investors see a candlestick that appears to signal a reversal and rush to give it a name.

But whether a candlestick is meaningful is determined not by its name, but by the market
structure in which it appears.

I expanded the chart of SiliconWorks Europe.

Although the stock had been declining for an entire month, it had not yet reached a major long-
term support level. There was still an unfilled price gap overhead, while the nearest high-volume
trading area was located even lower. More importantly, although the session produced a long
lower shadow, trading volume did not expand significantly.

What did that tell us?

It suggested that buying interest had indeed appeared near the lows, but the strength of that
buying was not necessarily sufficient.

I turned to Ethan.

"What did this candlestick actually tell you?"

He answered,

"During the session, the bears pushed the price lower, but the bulls managed to pull it back before
the close."

"Very good," I said. "That's a description of market behavior. Now tell me—what did it not tell
you?"

He thought for a moment.

"It didn't tell me that the trend had already reversed."

"Exactly."

A long lower shadow tells us only that buyers stepped in near the day's low.

By itself, it cannot prove that the trend has reversed.

It could simply reflect short-term bargain hunting.

It could be caused by short covering.

It could represent temporary buying support at a technical level.

Or it could be nothing more than a brief pause within an ongoing decline.

If price continues higher the following day, breaks above the previous day's high, and is
supported by stronger trading volume, then the significance of that long lower shadow increases.

If, instead, the next day opens weak, sells off, and breaks below the low of the long lower shadow,
then the candlestick was nothing more than a failed rebound.

II. The Professor Erased Every Candlestick Name

That afternoon, SiliconWorks Europe staged a brief rally.

Ethan grew increasingly restless.

He kept switching between different chart timeframes, trying to determine whether the long lower
shadow was beginning to work.

I called him over to the whiteboard.

"Write down every candlestick pattern you know."

He looked surprised, but he did as I asked.

Hammer. / Doji. / Engulfing. / Shooting Star. / Morning Star. / Evening Star. / Marubozu. /
Spinning Top.

When he finished writing, he looked at me.

I picked up the whiteboard eraser and wiped away every single pattern name.

Ethan froze.

Then I wrote four new words on the board.

Open / High / Low / Close / Opening Price. / High Price. / Low Price. / Closing Price.

"Before you memorize the name of any candlestick," I said, "you must first understand what these
four prices are telling you."

The essence of a candlestick is actually very simple.

The opening price represents the price the market was willing to accept at the beginning of the
trading period.

The high price represents the highest level the buyers were able to push the price to.

The low price represents the lowest level the sellers managed to drive the price down to.

The closing price represents the price the market ultimately accepted at the end of that period.

The size of the real body reflects which side held the advantage at the close.

The length of the shadows shows that one side launched an attack but was pushed back by the
other.

The location of the close reveals who temporarily won the battle by the end of the session.

Therefore, a candlestick is not merely a shape.

It is the record of a battle.

"Now you're reading candlesticks instead of memorizing them."



III. The Same Candlestick Can Mean Completely Different Things in Different Locations

The following day, SiliconWorks Europe failed to continue its advance.

The stock opened slightly higher but quickly pulled back. By the afternoon, it had fallen below
the body of the previous day's long lower shadow and closed only slightly above the previous
day's low.

Ethan frowned.

"So... did that Hammer Candle fail?"

"Not completely," I replied.

"But it never received confirmation."

"So the location still wasn't good enough?"

"The location wasn't good enough, the trading volume wasn't convincing enough, and there was
no meaningful follow-through."

I drew the exact same long lower shadow in three different locations.

The second scenario: Midway through an existing downtrend, price has not yet reached any

The third scenario: During a high-level trading range, the long lower shadow reflects nothing

The very same long lower shadow carries completely different meanings.

Near a market bottom, it may indicate that panic selling has been absorbed.

In the middle of a downtrend, it may represent nothing more than a short-term rebound.

At high price levels, it may simply reflect increased volatility.

A candlestick must always be interpreted within the context of Market Structure.

IV. A Debate Triggered by a Long Upper Shadow

One week later, the research department gathered to discuss another stock.

It belonged to a luxury retail company, which I will refer to as Lumière Maison.

Lumière Maison had been climbing steadily and had recently reached a new intermediate high.
That morning, strong sales data drove the stock to gap higher, and the rally continued after the
open. At one point, it was up more than 6%.

But by the afternoon, the situation changed.

The stock retreated sharply from its intraday high and closed with a gain of only 1%, leaving a
long upper shadow on the daily chart. Trading volume expanded significantly.

A disagreement quickly emerged in the meeting room.

One research analyst believed it was nothing more than normal profit-taking and argued that the
company's fundamentals remained strong.

A trader disagreed.

He believed the candlestick reflected increasing selling pressure near the highs and warned that
caution was warranted.

Ethan studied the chart without rushing to offer an opinion.

I turned to him.

"What do you think?"

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 164 / 686>
This time, he didn't say,

"It's a Shooting Star."

Nor did he say,

"It's a top."

Instead, he answered,

"This candlestick tells us that buyers managed to push the price much higher during the session,
but by the close, sellers had erased most of those gains. Combined with the surge in trading
volume, it suggests there was significant disagreement at higher prices. It doesn't necessarily
mean the stock has topped, but it does tell us that blindly chasing the rally here would be unwise."

I nodded.

"Go one step further."

He continued,

"If the stock fails to break above the high of this long upper shadow over the next several
sessions—or even falls below today's low—it would suggest that selling pressure is gaining the
upper hand."

"But if price pulls back on lighter volume and later reclaims today's high, then this long upper
shadow may simply represent a temporary transfer of shares rather than the beginning of a
reversal."

This time, his answer was excellent.

He didn't treat the candlestick's name as the conclusion.

Instead, he described the market behavior, identified the conditions that mattered, and
acknowledged the uncertainty.

Professor's Whiteboard Session

Candlestick Behavior

Candlestick Behavior Analysis.

I did not ask him to memorize a single pattern name.

        A candlestick is not a symbol that predicts the future. It is evidence of how
        buyers and sellers fought during a specific period of time.

I. The Four Prices of a Candlestick

Every candlestick is built from four prices:

Open — the opening price.

High — the highest price.

Low — the lowest price.

Close — the closing price.

Together, these four prices form a complete record of the battle during a given period.

The opening price tells us where the battle began.

The high tells us how far buyers were able to advance.

The low tells us how far sellers managed to drive the market down.

The close tells us where the market ultimately accepted price by the end of the session.

If the close is near the high, buyers held the advantage at the end of that period.

If the close is near the low, sellers held the advantage.

If both the upper and lower shadows are long, it means that both buyers and sellers launched
strong attacks, resulting in significant disagreement.

If the real body is small, it means the opening and closing prices were close together, reflecting
temporary indecision.



II. What Does the Real Body Represent?

The larger the real body, the greater the difference between the opening and closing prices.

A large bullish candle indicates that buyers were clearly in control.

A large bearish candle indicates that sellers dominated the session.

A small real body suggests that buying and selling pressure were relatively balanced, leaving the
market without a clear direction.

However, the real body must always be interpreted within its market context.

A large bullish candle with expanding volume at a market bottom may indicate that institutional
buying is beginning to emerge.




                                                - 158 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 167 / 686>
A large bullish candle with heavy volume near the top of an extended advance may signal
emotional buying or even a buying climax.

A large bullish candle during an existing downtrend may be nothing more than a short-term
rebound.

Likewise, a large bearish candle during an established uptrend may simply represent a normal
pullback—or it could mark the beginning of a trend breakdown.

No candlestick should ever be evaluated in isolation.

III. What Do the Shadows Represent?

The shadows record attacks and counterattacks.

A long upper shadow tells us that price advanced during the session but was pushed back before
the close.



                                              - 159 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 168 / 686>
It reflects the presence of selling pressure overhead.

A long lower shadow tells us that price was driven lower during the session but recovered before
the close.

It reflects buying support emerging at lower prices.

However, shadows are not standalone trading signals.

A long upper shadow deserves greater attention when it appears at a high price level.

A long upper shadow during the early stages of a rebound from a market bottom may simply
represent a normal test of resistance.

A long lower shadow carries greater significance when it forms near a major support area.

A long lower shadow appearing in the middle of a downtrend may be nothing more than
temporary bargain hunting.

Shadows tell you that a battle took place.

They do not tell you that the war has already been won.



IV. Why Shouldn't a Doji Be Misused?

Ethan used to be especially fond of the Doji.

He believed that a Doji signaled an imminent trend reversal.

I told him,

"A Doji represents indecision, not an automatic reversal."

A Doji forms when the opening price and closing price are nearly the same, indicating that
neither buyers nor sellers gained a decisive advantage during that period.

But what happens next can go in either direction.

If a Doji appears after a prolonged uptrend, followed by heavy volume and a downside
breakdown, it may signal indecision at the top before a reversal.

If a Doji appears after a prolonged downtrend and is followed by a strong advance on increased
trading volume, it may represent bottoming action before a bullish reversal.

If a Doji appears in the middle of a sideways trading range, it may carry very little significance.

For this reason, a Doji requires confirmation more than almost any other candlestick pattern.

V. The Four Questions for Candlestick Analysis

From that day forward, whenever Ethan encountered an unusual candlestick, I asked him to
answer four questions before drawing any conclusions.

First, where did it appear?

Was it at a market bottom, near a top, in the middle of a trend, at a support level, or at a resistance
level?

The location changes its meaning entirely.

Second, what happened beforehand?

A long upper shadow after a prolonged rally is not the same as a long upper shadow after an
extended decline.




                                               - 161 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 170 / 686>
Context always matters.

Third, what did the trading volume tell you?

A high-volume long upper shadow, a low-volume long upper shadow at the highs, and a high-
volume long lower shadow near support all carry different implications.

Fourth, was there any confirmation afterward?

Without confirmation, a candlestick is merely an observation—not a complete trading signal.

Case Review

Why Couldn't the Hammer Candle in SiliconWorks Europe Be Bought Immediately?

Now let's return to SiliconWorks Europe.

At first, Ethan saw what appeared to be an almost textbook Hammer Candle with a long lower
shadow.


                                               - 162 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 171 / 686>
It did show that buyers stepped in during the session.

But why couldn't it be treated as an immediate buy signal?

First, it did not form at a clearly defined major support zone.

Second, the existing downtrend had not yet been broken.

Third, trading volume failed to expand significantly, making the buying interest less convincing.

Fourth, price failed to break above the previous day's high on the following session.

Fifth, the market never completed a structural confirmation.

Therefore, the correct interpretation of that candlestick was not "buy signal."

Instead, it was an observation signal.

It told us only that buying interest might be emerging.

It showed that sellers had encountered buying pressure after pushing prices lower during the
session.

Whether the trend would actually reverse still depended on future confirmation.

If price later broke above the previous day's high on expanding trading volume and reclaimed
short-term resistance, the significance of the long lower shadow would increase.

If price instead broke below the low of the long lower shadow, it would indicate that buying
support had failed, invalidating the original observation.

That is the essence of candlestick analysis.

A candlestick is not a conclusion.

It is one piece of evidence.

Chapter Trading Rules

That day, I asked Ethan to write down five trading rules.

First, do not trade the name of a candlestick. Analyze the behavior it represents.

Second, a long upper shadow reflects selling pressure overhead, while a long lower shadow
reflects buying support below. However, both require the proper market location and subsequent
confirmation before they become meaningful.

Third, a large bullish candle is not automatically bullish, and a large bearish candle is not
automatically bearish. Their significance depends on where they appear within the market
structure.




                                               - 163 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 172 / 686>
Fourth, a Doji represents indecision. It does not automatically signal a trend reversal.

Fifth, no single candlestick should ever be evaluated in isolation. It must always be analyzed
together with the trend, support and resistance levels, trading volume, and subsequent price action.

Then I added one more sentence:

        A candlestick tells you how one battle ended, but it cannot, by itself, tell
        you who will win the entire war.

Ethan wrote that sentence in his notebook.

After that day, he rarely asked me,

        "Is this a Hammer?"

        "Is this a Shooting Star?"

        "Is this a Doji?"

Instead, he began asking,

        "Which side became more aggressive at this level, and what does this
        candlestick reveal about the battle between buyers and sellers?"

That was real progress.



Chapter Summary

He no longer viewed them as patterns whose names needed to be memorized.

Instead, he learned to read them as records of market behavior.

He came to understand that:

       The High shows the furthest point buyers were able to advance.
       The Low shows the deepest level sellers managed to push prices.

       The real body reflects which side held control by the close.
       The shadows record attacks and counterattacks between buyers and sellers.

He also realized that the same candlestick can carry completely different meanings depending on
