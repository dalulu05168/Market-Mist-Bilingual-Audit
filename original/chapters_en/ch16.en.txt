The Bull Market's Champagne Tower: How Rising Prices Make Investors Forget
Risk


The most dangerous aspect of a bull market

is not the rise itself.

There is nothing inherently wrong with rising prices.

Markets advance,

valuations expand,

investors become more willing to take risks,

and people make money.

All of these are perfectly normal parts of the financial markets.

The real danger is this:

A bull market changes people's personalities.

A cautious investor becomes confident.

A confident investor becomes bold.

A bold investor becomes reckless.

And a reckless investor begins believing

that they have finally mastered the market.

A bull market is like a towering champagne fountain.

Those standing at the very top

raise their glasses first,

make the most noise,

and are the quickest to believe

that the celebration will never end.

Those below watch them making money,

Professor's Whiteboard Session

Triple Top Pattern

That evening,

the topic of Ethan's whiteboard lesson was:

The Triple Top Pattern.

A Triple Top is less common than a Double Top,

but once confirmed,

it generally carries a stronger bearish implication.

Its defining characteristic

is not simply that three peaks appear at roughly the same level.

Its true significance lies here:

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 366 / 686>
Price attempts to break through the same resistance zone three separate times,

yet each attempt is rejected by the market.

This tells us

that supply above the market remains exceptionally strong,

while buying momentum is gradually being exhausted.



I. How a Triple Top Forms

A typical Triple Top

usually develops through six stages.

Stage One: The Existing Uptrend

A Triple Top

almost always appears

after a prolonged advance.

Without a meaningful prior uptrend,

it should not be considered

a major topping pattern.

Stage Two: The First Peak

Price rallies

to a new high.

Trading Volume

is usually relatively heavy,

and market sentiment remains highly optimistic.

At this stage,

it is far too early

to conclude that a top has formed.

The first peak




                                              - 358 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 367 / 686>
simply marks the beginning of the observation process.

Stage Three: The Pullback Forms the Neckline

Price retreats

from the first peak

and eventually finds Support.

That low

becomes the potential Neckline.

Stage Four: The Second Peak

Price once again challenges

the previous high,

but fails to produce

a convincing breakout.

If Trading Volume

is lighter

than during the first peak,

it suggests

that buying momentum

is already beginning to weaken.

Stage Five: The Third Peak

Price makes

a third attempt

to break through

the same resistance zone.

If Trading Volume

declines even further,

or if long upper shadows appear,




                                            - 359 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 368 / 686>
or if price rallies intraday before reversing lower,

these are all signs

that demand

may be becoming severely exhausted.

Stage Six: Neckline Breakdown Confirmation

Price declines

from the third peak

and breaks below

the Neckline.

Only then

is the Triple Top

officially confirmed.

Until the Neckline breaks,

it remains

nothing more

than a potential Triple Top.



II. What Is the Difference Between a Triple Top and a Double Top?

A Double Top

consists of

two failed attempts

to break higher.

A Triple Top

consists of

three failed attempts.

A Triple Top

III. How Should Trading Volume Be Interpreted?

Within a Triple Top,

Trading Volume

typically follows several important characteristics.

       The first peak forms on relatively heavy Trading Volume, reflecting strong buying
        enthusiasm.
       The second peak forms on lighter Trading Volume, indicating weakening demand.
       The third peak forms on even lighter Trading Volume, suggesting increasing buyer
        exhaustion.
       The Neckline Breakdown occurs on expanding Trading Volume, reflecting concentrated
        selling pressure.
       The Retest of the Neckline develops on declining Trading Volume, showing that buyers
        have failed to regain control.

These are not absolute rules,

but they occur frequently.

Progressively lighter Trading Volume

during all three rallies

shows that although price repeatedly returns

to the same highs,

market participation

is steadily declining.

When that occurs,

a subsequent Neckline Breakdown

deserves particularly close attention.

I asked Ethan

First, there must be a clear prior uptrend.

Second, the three peaks should form within approximately the same resistance zone. They do not
have to be exactly equal.

Third, Trading Volume should ideally decline with each successive rally.

Fourth, the pattern is not confirmed until price breaks below the Neckline.

That is also a mistake.

A topping pattern

Chapter Trading Rules

That day,

I asked Ethan

to write down seven trading rules.

First, a Triple Top usually develops after a prolonged uptrend.

Second, the three peaks do not have to be exactly the same height. What matters is that all three
fail to break through the same resistance zone.

Third, progressively declining Trading Volume across the three rallies is an important sign of
buyer exhaustion.

Fourth, before the Neckline is broken, the pattern is only a potential Triple Top.

Fifth, a Triple Top is confirmed only after price breaks below the Neckline on expanding Trading
Volume.

Sixth, if the subsequent Retest fails, it confirms that former Support has become new Resistance.

Seventh, in a bull market, a compelling story can never replace proper risk management.

        A bull market does not reduce risk simply because you are making money.

Chapter Summary

In this chapter,

Ethan Carter

learned how to recognize

the Triple Top pattern.

He came to understand that:

A market top

does not necessarily begin

with bad news.

Nor does it necessarily appear

because the company itself

has become weaker.

More often,

a top develops

because price repeatedly fails

to gain market acceptance.

The essence of a Triple Top

is not simply

three mountain-like peaks.

Its essence

is three consecutive failures.

At the first peak,

the market remains enthusiastic.

At the second,

At the third,

buying momentum

is visibly exhausted.

Only when price

breaks below the Neckline

is the pattern

truly confirmed.

From this chapter onward,

Ethan no longer focused

only on the upside potential

during a bull market.

Instead,

he began asking himself:

          Can this stock still make new highs?
          Is Trading Volume becoming weaker with each rally?
          Where is the Neckline?
          After a breakdown, can price reclaim it?
          Has market consensus become excessively one-sided?

A bull market

brings excitement.

But it can also

dull investors' awareness of risk.

A mature investor

must know where the exit is

before the last glass of champagne

has been poured.

In the next chapter,

the market will give Ethan

an entirely different experience.

If a bull market

is like a champagne tower,

then a bear market

is like an elevator.

Markets climb

one step at a time.

But they often fall

like an elevator

with its cables suddenly cut—

so fast

that investors

have almost no time to react.
