Chapter 21
Earnings Season on Trial: Why the Market Rejects Good Numbers


Earnings season is like a public trial.

Companies step onto the stage and present their revenue, profits, gross margins, cash flow, order
backlog, forward guidance, and management's explanations.

Analysts sit in the front row, rapidly updating their financial models.

Fund managers sit behind them, reassessing their portfolio allocations.

Traders watch the order flow, studying how capital responds.

Retail investors open the headlines wanting only one answer:

Good or bad?

But the market never judges only the past.

What it truly judges is the future.

That is also what makes earnings season so confusing for many investors.

Some companies report outstanding results, yet their stock prices fall.

Others miss earnings expectations, yet their shares rally.

Some companies report slowing revenue growth, but their stock surges higher.

Others deliver earnings well above expectations, only to gap higher at the open before selling off
throughout the day.

Ethan Carter first truly understood this lesson while studying a European payment technology
company.

For the purposes of this book, I will call it Mercury Payments.

Mercury Payments provides payment solutions for European small and medium-sized businesses,
cross-border e-commerce merchants, and digital subscription platforms. Its services include
online payment processing, cross-border settlement, fraud prevention systems, merchant lending,
and data analytics.

During the previous two years, it had become one of the market's favorite growth stocks.

Revenue was growing rapidly.

Profit margins continued to improve.

Customer retention remained high.

Digital payment adoption still had considerable room to expand.

Management also excelled at telling a compelling growth story.

The stock had climbed from €38 to €72, and later advanced again from €72 to €104.

This was not a speculative company with weak fundamentals.

Its growth was genuine.

The problem was this:

The market had already paid a very high price for that growth.

Before the earnings announcement, Mercury Payments had risen steadily for three consecutive
months.

Most analysts expected another strong earnings report.

Across social media, many investors described it as "one of Europe's highest-quality fintech
platforms."

Several investment funds had also disclosed larger positions during the previous quarter.

In other words, long before the earnings release, the market had already cast its vote.

Ethan was very bullish on the company.

And he had every reason to be.

Fundamentally, it was an outstanding business.

Technically, price remained above both the 20-Day and 50-Day Moving Averages.

Its Relative Strength was clearly outperforming the FinTech ETF.

Previous breakouts had also been supported by healthy Trading Volume.

Based on everything available before earnings were released, it appeared to be a textbook
example of a leading growth stock.

But this is exactly where earnings season becomes dangerous.

The market is not asking whether a company is good.

It is asking whether the company is better than what the market has already expected.

I. Good Earnings, Bad Reaction

Mercury Payments released its earnings after the market closed on Wednesday.

On the surface, the results looked outstanding.

Quarterly revenue increased 28% year over year.

Adjusted profit margins improved.

Earnings per share exceeded analysts' expectations.

Merchant payment volume continued growing.

Management reaffirmed its full-year revenue guidance.

The financial headlines were equally positive:

        "Mercury Payments Beats Earnings Expectations."

        "Revenue Growth Remains Strong."

        "Digital Payment Demand Stays Resilient."

That evening, Ethan sent me a message.

        "Professor, Mercury's earnings look excellent. It could gap higher
        tomorrow."

I replied with only one sentence.

        "Tomorrow, watch how the market delivers its verdict."

The following morning, Mercury Payments did indeed gap higher.

The previous day's closing price had been €104.

It opened immediately at €112.

Shortly after the open, it briefly reached €116.

Someone on the trading desk said,

        "The market clearly approves of the earnings."

Ethan was beginning to feel excited.

Standing beside him, I asked,

        "Has the market accepted the earnings—or is this only an opening gap?"

He paused for a moment.

        "We can't know yet. We need to see whether price can hold."

He no longer treated a strong opening as the market's final verdict.

After 10:00 a.m., the chart began changing.

Price stopped making further progress.

A leading growth stock had reported excellent earnings.

It gapped higher by more than 8% at the open.

At one point it was up more than 11% intraday.

Yet it finished the session in negative territory.

This is a classic Earnings Gap Failure.

A failed earnings gap higher.

II. Why Does the Market Sell Good News?

After the market closed, the research department held an emergency review meeting.

The FinTech analyst still believed that the long-term quality of Mercury Payments remained
intact.

He listed several positive factors:

Revenue growth remained strong.

Profit margins continued improving.

Merchant transaction volume remained healthy.

The company had not lowered its full-year guidance.

But the trading desk offered a different perspective.

        "The good news had already been priced in."

That sentence is extremely important.

Before earnings, Mercury Payments had already rallied significantly.

Market expectations had become exceptionally high.

Many investors had bought the stock in advance, expecting strong earnings.

Once those earnings were actually released, if the results were merely good, rather than far better
than expected, early buyers had every reason to lock in profits.

This was not because the company had become a poor business.

It was because the market no longer had a new reason to pay an even higher price.

More subtly, during the earnings conference call, management mentioned several additional
points:

Marketing expenses could increase during the second half of the year.

Competitive pressure on certain cross-border transaction fees was increasing.

Growth in the merchant lending business would become more conservative.

None of these comments represented major negative news.

But for a richly valued growth stock, even small imperfections can become an excuse for
investors to take profits.

Ethan said,

        "So the market isn't saying the company is bad. It's saying the current
        price had already become too optimistic."

"Exactly."

I asked another question.

        "What does today's Trading Volume tell you?"

He studied the volume bars.

After thinking for a moment, he answered slowly.

        "A huge amount of shares changed hands near the highs. Early buyers

I nodded.

That is one of the most common topping behaviors during earnings season.

Good News, Bad Reaction.

The news is positive.

The price reaction is not.

III. Volume Climax: Heavy Activity Hides Heavy Distribution

The Trading Volume on Mercury Payments' earnings day was extraordinary.

This was not simply a high-volume session.

It was a Volume Climax.

A Volume Climax should never be interpreted as bullish or bearish based on volume alone.

It must always be evaluated together with price location and the final candlestick.

If a stock surges on exceptionally high Trading Volume after breaking out from a long-term base,
the Volume Climax may indicate aggressive institutional buying.




                                              - 466 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 475 / 686>
If a stock rallies to new highs on extremely heavy Trading Volume but then reverses sharply
lower, the Volume Climax may signal institutional distribution.

If a stock forms a long lower shadow near a panic low on massive Trading Volume, it may
represent selling exhaustion followed by strong buying interest.

If a stock gaps higher on good earnings after a prolonged advance, forms a long upper shadow,
and closes weakly, investors should be alert to distribution.

Mercury Payments belonged to the last category.

After a long advance, it used positive earnings as the catalyst for a gap higher.

The opening rally attracted a large number of momentum buyers.

Yet price failed to continue advancing.

Instead, it declined steadily throughout the day.

By the close, it had fallen below the previous day's closing price.

This tells us that exceptionally heavy Trading Volume was unable to keep price at higher levels.

Buying demand was absorbed by aggressive selling.

Ethan said,

        "Trading Volume was huge, but the closing price was terrible. That means
        the sellers were stronger."

"Exactly."

        "It feels very similar to a false breakout."

        "The underlying principle is the same," I replied. "The only difference is
        that this time, the catalyst was the earnings report."

IV. The Second Day Matters Even More

The long upper shadow on earnings day was already a serious warning sign.

But I did not allow Ethan to reach a final conclusion immediately.

Instead, I said,

        "Tomorrow will be even more important."

He asked,

        "Why?"

        "Because the first day may simply reflect emotional volatility. The second
        day tells you whether the market is willing to buy the stock back."

After an Earnings Gap Failure, the following trading day generally leads to one of three outcomes.

First, price quickly recovers the high of the earnings-day reversal, suggesting that the first day's
volatility was simply an intense exchange of ownership.

Second, price stabilizes near the previous day's closing level and enters a period of consolidation
while the market reassesses the situation.

Third, price continues declining, breaks below a key Support level, and confirms that the market's
post-earnings reaction has turned negative.

Mercury Payments followed the third path.

On the second day, it opened slightly lower.

During the morning session, it attempted to rebound toward €104, but sellers quickly pushed it
back down.

By midday, price had fallen below €101.

Breaking below €100 signaled that the failed earnings gap was no longer just a one-day emotional

This was the point at which the post-earnings top reversal became much clearer.

Over the following two weeks, Mercury Payments continued to decline.

It fell from around €104 to €96.

Then it dropped to €91.

Eventually, it approached €86.

Nothing disastrous had happened to the company.

There was no accounting fraud.

No major customer losses.

No management resignations.

No severe regulatory penalties.

Yet the stock price kept falling.

Ethan found this difficult to understand.

        "The company's fundamentals haven't deteriorated enough to justify this
        kind of decline."

A great company may remain a great company after an Earnings Gap Failure.

It becomes a poor trade because the market priced in the good news too early, too completely,

Professor's Whiteboard Session

Earnings Gap Failure / Volume Climax / Top Reversal

That evening, the topic of my whiteboard session with Ethan was:

Earnings Gap Failure, Volume Climax, and Top Reversals.

The first sentence I wrote on the board was:

        An earnings report reveals the past. Price reflects expectations.

The mistake investors make most often during earnings season is assuming that earnings results
and stock price reactions should always move in the same direction.

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 482 / 686>
Good earnings should mean a higher stock price.

Bad earnings should mean a lower stock price.

In reality, the market is far more complicated than that.

The market trades the gap between expectations and reality.

If expectations were originally very low, an earnings report that is simply less bad than expected
may still drive the stock higher.

If expectations were already extremely high, an earnings report that is merely good may still send
the stock lower.

Therefore, the most important questions during earnings season are not:

        "What did the company report?"

Instead, ask:

        "What was the market expecting?"

        "How much of that expectation had already been priced in?"

        "How did institutional capital react after the announcement?"



I. Four Types of Earnings Reactions

There are four common market reactions following an earnings announcement.

First: Good Numbers, Good Reaction

The company reports results above expectations.

The stock rises on expanding Trading Volume and holds its gains into the close.

This is the most straightforward bullish reaction.

Second: Good Numbers, Bad Reaction

The company delivers strong earnings, but the stock rallies initially before reversing lower, or
even closes below the previous day's close.

This often indicates that market expectations had already become too optimistic, or that
institutional investors are taking profits on the good news.

Third: Bad Numbers, Bad Reaction

The company misses expectations.



                                               - 474 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 483 / 686>
The stock declines sharply on heavy Trading Volume.

This is the most direct bearish outcome.

Fourth: Bad Numbers, Good Reaction

The company reports disappointing results, yet the stock opens lower before recovering
strongly—or even finishes higher.

This may indicate that the bad news had already been fully priced in, or that the market believes
the worst is already behind the company.

II. What Is an Earnings Gap Failure?




                                              - 475 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 484 / 686>
An Earnings Gap Failure occurs when a company reports earnings, the stock gaps sharply higher
at the open, but fails to hold those gains and eventually falls back into the gap—or even below the
previous day's closing price.

It typically has several characteristics.

First, the stock has already advanced significantly before the earnings announcement.

Second, market expectations are unusually high.

Third, price gaps higher immediately after the earnings release.

Fourth, heavy selling pressure appears during the trading session.

Fifth, Trading Volume expands dramatically.

Sixth, the stock closes with a long upper shadow or finishes below the previous day's close.

Seventh, price later breaks below short-term Support.

The key point of an Earnings Gap Failure is not that the earnings report was poor.

In many cases, the earnings report is actually very good.

The real issue is that the market refuses to accept a higher price.



III. What Is a Volume Climax at the Top?

A Volume Climax refers to a trading session with exceptionally heavy Trading Volume.

However, it must always be interpreted within its market context.

A Volume Climax at the top typically occurs after a prolonged uptrend.

If price rallies during the session but then reverses lower, forming a long upper shadow or a large
bearish candlestick on heavy Trading Volume, it suggests that a significant transfer of shares is
taking place near the highs.

This may indicate:

       Early investors taking profits.
       Institutional investors selling into abundant liquidity.
       Momentum traders buying near the top.
       Short-term market enthusiasm reaching an extreme.
       Market expectations becoming overly stretched.

A Volume Climax is not automatically bearish.




                                               - 476 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 485 / 686>
But when it appears near a market top together with a failed candlestick pattern, investors should
exercise extreme caution.



IV. Confirmation Requirements for an Earnings Top Reversal

I asked Ethan to memorize six confirmation conditions.

First, the stock has already risen substantially before earnings.

Second, market expectations and valuation have become highly optimistic.

Third, the stock gaps higher after earnings but fails to hold those gains.

Fourth, Trading Volume expands dramatically.

Fifth, the stock closes poorly, forming either a long upper shadow or a close below the previous
day's closing price.

Sixth, price later breaks below a key Support level or fails during a retest.

The sixth condition is the most important.

A one-day reversal is only a warning.

A breakdown that follows is the confirmation.

V. What Should You Watch After an Earnings Report?

After an earnings announcement, don't focus only on the news headlines.

Instead, pay attention to five things.

First, watch the opening direction.

Did the stock gap higher or gap lower?

Second, watch the intraday price action.

After a gap higher, can price continue rising?

After a gap lower, does buying demand begin to appear?

Third, watch where the stock closes.

Does it finish near the high, the middle, or the low of the day's range?

Fourth, watch the Trading Volume.

When volume expands, is it driving price higher—or is it simply being absorbed by selling
pressure?


                                                 - 477 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 486 / 686>
Fifth, watch the next two to three trading days.

Does the market's reaction continue?

Does price break below—or reclaim—key technical levels?

Is any retest successful?

Trading earnings is not simply about trading the numbers.

It is about trading expectations, positioning, and the market's price reaction.



Case Review

Why Did the Market Reject Mercury Payments?

Now let's return to Mercury Payments.

Why is it such an excellent earnings-season case study?

Because the earnings themselves were not disappointing.

Revenue exceeded expectations.

Profit exceeded expectations.

Management did not lower its guidance.

The business continued to grow.

Yet the market still sold the stock.

The reasons were not limited to a single factor.

First, the stock had already advanced significantly before earnings.

Second, market expectations had become exceptionally high.

Third, the company's valuation had already reflected strong future growth.

Fourth, management mentioned higher operating costs and increasing competitive pressure.

Fifth, the earnings gap higher attracted momentum buyers.

Sixth, Trading Volume exploded near the highs, yet price failed to hold its gains.

Seventh, the stock closed below the previous day's closing price.

Eighth, it continued falling the next day, breaking below the important €100 Support level.

Ninth, the subsequent retest of €100 failed, turning former Support into new Resistance.

All of this suggests that the market was not rejecting the company's underlying business.

It was repricing its growth expectations.

During his review, Ethan wrote a single sentence:

        "The report was good. The reaction was bad. The reaction wins."

The earnings report was good.

The market's reaction was bad.

And in the end, the reaction is what matters.

Trading Rules for This Chapter

That day, I asked Ethan to write down seven trading rules.

Rule 1: An earnings report reflects the past. The stock price reflects expectations.

Rule 2: Good earnings do not always lead to higher prices, and poor earnings do not always lead
to lower prices.

Rule 3: The greater the advance before earnings, the higher market expectations usually become.

Rule 4: A gap higher that fails to hold is an important warning sign of an Earnings Gap Failure.

Rule 5: A Volume Climax near the highs, combined with a long upper shadow, should raise
concerns about institutional distribution.

Rule 6: A one-day reversal is only a warning. A subsequent break below Support is the true
confirmation.

Rule 7: After earnings, don't read only the numbers. Read the market's price reaction even more
carefully.

I then added one final sentence.

        "The market is not a teacher grading companies based on effort."

        "It asks only one question: Is this result worth today's price?"

Ethan copied the sentence into his notebook.

Chapter Summary

In this chapter, Ethan Carter came to understand the harsh reality of earnings season.

He learned that good earnings do not guarantee a rising stock price.

A great company does not necessarily represent a safe trade.

When market expectations become excessively high, even good news can become a reason to sell.

The enormous Trading Volume on earnings day may simply represent an ideal opportunity for
institutional investors to exchange ownership.

A failed gap higher after earnings is an important warning signal.

A subsequent break below Support confirms that the market structure has weakened.

From this chapter onward, Ethan no longer asked only:

        "Were the earnings good?"

Instead, he began asking:

        "What had the market already expected?"

        "How much had the stock already risen before earnings?"

        "Could price hold after the gap?"

        "Was Trading Volume driving the advance—or being absorbed by selling
        pressure?"

        "Did the market follow through on the second day?"

        "Has key Support been broken?"

Earnings season is not simply an exam of good or bad results.

It is the market's judgment of expectations.

In the next chapter, Ethan begins one of the most important lessons in trading:

Before buying any stock, ask yourself five questions.

Because real trading does not begin when you click the Buy button.

It begins in the moment before you place the order.
