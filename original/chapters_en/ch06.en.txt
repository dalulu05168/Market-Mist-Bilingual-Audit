Chapter 6
Support and Resistance: The Footprints Left by Market Memory.


Ethan Carter did not truly understand support and resistance in a classroom.

Nor did he learn it from a technical analysis textbook.

He learned it while watching a stock that seemed impossible to push any lower.

It was a European consumer goods company.

For the sake of this story, I will call it Maison Verde.

Maison Verde was not one of the market's most exciting companies.

It had no artificial intelligence story.

No clean energy theme.

And it was not the kind of stock that would surge twenty percent because of a single order
announcement.

Its core businesses were traditional: premium home furnishings, fragrances, lifestyle retail, and an
online membership subscription business.

Companies like this tend to share one characteristic.

During bull markets, they are rarely the fastest performers.

During bear markets, they are rarely the hardest hit.

But when investors begin searching for stable cash flow and durable brand moats, they often
return to the attention of institutional investors.

At first, Ethan did not like companies like Maison Verde.

To him, everything about the company seemed slow.

The price moved slowly.

Earnings grew slowly.

News arrived slowly.

Even the level of market attention was slow.

Young research analysts often prefer stocks that can validate their analysis quickly.

Research it today.

Watch it rise tomorrow.

See a breakout today.

Make a profit tomorrow.

Yet many of the market's most important signals are hidden within slow, repetitive, and
seemingly boring price behavior.

Maison Verde was one of those stocks.

During the previous six months, its share price had repeatedly declined toward €38.

Each time it approached that level, buyers stepped in.

The first visit to €38 produced a rebound.

The second visit produced another rebound.

The third visit came during a period of obvious market fear.

Yet even then, price still failed to break decisively below the level.

Staring at the chart, Ethan finally asked,

"Professor, why does it always bounce from this area?

Is someone supporting the price?"

I was reviewing an industry inventory report.

Hearing his question, I looked up.

"You think someone is defending the stock?"

"It certainly looks that way," he replied.

"Every time the price approaches €38, buying appears, and trading volume also increases."

"Then why do you think this level is important?"

"Maybe it's where institutions built their positions."

"Perhaps," I answered.

"But don't rush to explain everything as institutional price support.

Some areas become important not because a mysterious force is controlling the market there...

...but because a great many people's memories are concentrated at that level."

Ethan frowned.

"Price has memory?"

"No," I said.

"Price doesn't have memory. People do."



I. The Level That Refused to Break

I asked Ethan to enlarge the daily chart of Maison Verde.

The area around €38 stood out immediately.

During the previous six months, price had approached that region three separate times.

Each visit had been followed by a rebound.

The first rebound was relatively modest.

The second was much stronger.

The third occurred while the overall market remained weak, yet Maison Verde still defended the
same area.

Ethan drew a horizontal line across the chart.

"That's the support level."

"It's a support zone," I corrected.

"A support zone?"

"Don't think of support as an exact number.

The market is not a mathematics exam.

Institutions don't automatically buy because the price reaches exactly €38.00.

Nor do they abandon the trade simply because it falls to €37.99.

What usually matters is an entire price zone."

I wrote a sentence on the whiteboard.

Support Zone, Not Support Line

Support is a zone, not a single line.

Many investors make technical analysis far more precise than it really is.

They say:

"€42.50 is resistance."

"If price breaks below €37.98, it's over."

"If it breaks above €42.51, it's going to explode."

That kind of thinking is dangerous in real trading.

Markets contain noise.

Large orders sweep through the order book.

Prices briefly pierce important levels during the trading session.

False breakouts occur.

Temporary liquidity shortages create sudden price spikes.

Professional investors focus on areas, not decimal points.

Maison Verde's support was not exactly €38.

It was a trading zone roughly between €37.50 and €38.50.

Within that area existed the cost basis of previous buyers.

The valuation judgments of long-term investors.

Potential accumulation plans from institutional capital.

And technical buying from short-term traders.

Together, these behaviors created the support zone.

"Yes.

"Many people are also unwilling to sell."

That perspective is often overlooked.

A market finds support at a particular price area not only because buyers become more aggressive.

It may also be because sellers become less willing to sell.

When price falls into an area the market considers relatively inexpensive, those who intended to
sell begin hesitating.

Meanwhile, investors waiting to buy become increasingly active.

Buying increases.

Selling decreases.

Naturally, price becomes more likely to rebound.

Support is not created by buyers alone.

It is the result of buyers becoming stronger while sellers become weaker at the same time.

II. Why Does Support Fail?

A few days later, Maison Verde once again drifted back toward €38.

This time, Ethan was noticeably more cautious.

Instead of immediately saying the stock was a buy, he began observing the trading volume, the
overall market environment, and the price reaction.

At that time, the broader European market was weak.

The consumer sector was also under pressure after several retail companies lowered their full-
year sales guidance.

Maison Verde itself had released no negative news.

However, sentiment toward the entire industry had deteriorated.

The price fell to €38.20.

Then €38.00.

Later during the session, it briefly touched €37.70.

Watching the screen, Ethan asked,

"Can we buy here?"

"What do you think?" I replied.

"It's reached the support zone."

"Reaching a support zone only tells you that you've arrived at an area worth watching.

It does not automatically mean it's time to buy."

"Then what should I look for?"

"The reaction."

That is the single most important word in support trading.

Reaction.

The support zone itself is not a reason to buy.

What matters is how price behaves after reaching that support zone.

If price reaches support, trading volume expands moderately, a long lower shadow appears, and
price rebounds to close back above the upper boundary of the support zone, it suggests buyers are
beginning to absorb the selling pressure.

If price reaches support on heavy volume but fails to rebound—instead continuing to drift
lower—it suggests selling pressure may be stronger than buying demand.

If price briefly breaks below support during the session but quickly recovers, it may simply be a
false breakdown.

If price breaks below support on expanding volume and closes beneath the support zone,
investors should become highly alert.

The support may have failed.

Ethan stared at the intraday chart.

At 11:00 a.m., Maison Verde fell below €37.50.

Trading volume began increasing.

I could see the tension in Ethan's eyes.

"Has the support broken?"

"It has broken intraday," I replied.

"But it's still too early to reach a conclusion."

By 2:00 p.m., price had recovered to around €38.

Ethan let out a quiet sigh of relief.

"So this could be a false breakdown?"

"Possibly."

By the closing bell, Maison Verde finished at €38.30, leaving behind a long lower shadow.

III. Resistance: The Market's Ceiling

Several weeks later, Maison Verde rebounded from around €38 to €44.

Ethan became excited.

"If it can break above €44, it could begin a new uptrend."

"Why €44?" I asked.

"Because every previous rebound failed there."

"So what does that area represent?"

"A resistance zone."

"And why does resistance exist?"

This time, Ethan did not answer the way he would have in the first chapter by simply saying,
"Because price couldn't go any higher."

Instead, he replied,

"There may be investors who bought near that level before and became trapped.

Now that price has returned close to their cost basis, they want to sell.

There may also be short-term traders who bought near the support zone and are now taking
profits as price reaches resistance.

In addition, some institutions may believe the stock is no longer inexpensive at this valuation and
therefore have little interest in adding more shares."

I smiled.

"Very good.

You're beginning to explain price through market behavior."

The nature of resistance is remarkably similar to support.

It is not a mysterious line.

A resistance zone is simply an area where selling pressure becomes concentrated.

That selling pressure may come from trapped investors.

It may come from profit-taking.

It may come from institutional distribution.

Or it may simply exist because a large number of traders are watching exactly the same technical

As price approaches resistance, buyers become more cautious.

Sellers become more aggressive.

Buying weakens.

Selling increases.

As a result, price becomes more likely to pull back.

"So if it breaks above €44, can we buy it?"

Price consolidated around €44 for three days.

On the first day, it rallied and then faded.

On the second day, it traded sideways within a narrow range.

On the third day, trading volume expanded, and the stock closed above €44.50.

Ethan did not immediately call it a buy.

He waited.

On the fourth day, price pulled back modestly toward €44.

Trading volume contracted noticeably.

The stock never fell back below the resistance zone.

On the fifth day, price resumed moving higher.

Only then did Ethan say,

"The old resistance may have become the new support."

"Now you're finally starting to read charts like a research analyst."

IV. Resistance Becomes Support, Support Becomes Resistance

One of the most fascinating aspects of support and resistance is that they can exchange roles.

When price repeatedly fails to break above a resistance zone, that area represents concentrated
selling pressure.



                                                - 114 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 123 / 686>
However, if price breaks through that resistance on expanding volume and later pulls back
without falling below it, the market begins redefining that area.

Those who wanted to sell may have already finished selling.

Those who had been waiting on the sidelines may now become willing buyers after seeing the
breakout.

Short sellers may be forced to cover their positions.

Those who chased the breakout may hope for a pullback to buy at a better price.

As a result, old resistance becomes new support.

The reverse is equally true.

When price breaks below a long-established support zone, investors who bought within that area
become trapped.

If price later rebounds back toward the former support, many of those investors may choose to
sell simply to break even.

New selling pressure appears.

Buyers become more cautious.

As a result, old support becomes new resistance.

That is how market memory evolves.

Professor's Whiteboard Session

Support and Resistance

That evening, the topic of Ethan's whiteboard lesson was:

Support and Resistance.

This is one of the most fundamental concepts in technical analysis.

Yet it is also one of the easiest concepts to draw incorrectly, apply incorrectly, and misunderstand.

Many investors treat support and resistance as magical prediction lines.

They believe that price must rise after reaching support and must fall after reaching resistance.

That is an extremely dangerous misunderstanding.

Support and resistance are not prediction tools.

They are areas for observing changes in market behavior.



I. What Is Support?

Support is a price area where, after a decline, buying pressure begins increasing or selling
pressure begins weakening, causing price to stop falling—or even begin rebounding.

Support is usually created by four primary forces.

First, Cost Basis Support.

Many investors previously bought shares within that area.

When price returns, they are willing to add to their positions—or at least unwilling to sell.

Second, Valuation Support.

Some long-term investors believe the stock has become attractive at that price and begin
accumulating shares gradually.

Third, Technical Support.

Large numbers of traders recognize the same technical area, such as a previous low, the bottom of
a trading range, or a major moving average.


                                               - 118 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 127 / 686>
Fourth, Psychological Support.

Round numbers, historical lows, and important price platforms all influence investor psychology.

Regardless of where support originates, however, it must always be confirmed by price reaction.



II. What Is Resistance?

Resistance is a price area where, after an advance, selling pressure begins increasing or buying
pressure begins weakening, causing price to stop rising—or even begin declining.

Resistance is usually created by four primary forces.

First, Trapped Investors.

Those who bought near previous highs may decide to sell once price returns close to their cost
basis.

Second, Profit-Taking.

Investors who bought at lower prices choose to realize their gains after a meaningful advance.

Third, Valuation Pressure.

Institutions may believe the stock has reached a fair—or even expensive—valuation and therefore
become reluctant to buy additional shares.

Fourth, Technical Selling.

Large numbers of traders reduce their positions as price approaches previous highs, the upper
boundary of a trading range, or a major long-term resistance line.

Whether resistance is ultimately broken has nothing to do with how we draw the line.

It depends entirely on whether buyers possess enough strength to absorb the available selling
pressure.

III. How to Draw Support and Resistance Correctly

Later, I asked Ethan to follow three rules whenever he drew support and resistance.

Rule One: Draw Zones, Not Exact Price Levels

Market prices are filled with normal fluctuations and noise.

Support and resistance are usually price zones, not precise numbers.

Rule Two: Give Priority to Areas That Have Been Tested Repeatedly




                                              - 119 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 128 / 686>
The more times a price area has been tested, the stronger the market's memory tends to become.

However, there is an important exception.

If a support zone is tested over and over again while each rebound becomes progressively weaker,
it may indicate that buying demand is gradually being exhausted.

Rule Three: Combine Support and Resistance with Volume and Price Reaction

Simply drawing lines on a chart has very little value.

What truly matters is what happens after price reaches those areas.

Does price break above resistance on expanding volume?

Does it pull back on contracting volume?

Does it leave a long lower shadow before rebounding?

Does it break below support on heavy volume?

Does a rebound fail after returning to the former support?

These reactions—not the lines themselves—provide the real evidence.

IV. Common Mistakes When Using Support and Resistance

Although support and resistance appear simple, beginners frequently make four common
mistakes.

Mistake One: Believing Support Will Always Hold

No support level remains valid forever.

Support is an observation zone, not a guarantee.

If price breaks below support on expanding volume and the subsequent rebound fails, it is time to
accept that the support has failed.

Mistake Two: Believing Resistance Can Never Be Broken

Resistance is not permanent either.

If price breaks above resistance on expanding volume and later retests it successfully, the former
resistance may become new support.

Mistake Three: Drawing Too Many Lines

Some investors open a chart and cover it with support lines, resistance lines, trendlines, moving
averages, and price channels.

Eventually the chart looks highly sophisticated.

Yet they no longer know which line actually matters.

In reality, only a few price areas are truly important.

The more obvious the level, the more times it has been confirmed, and the stronger the
accompanying volume, the more attention it deserves.

Mistake Four: Ignoring the Market Environment

Exactly the same support level can have very different meanings under different market
conditions.

Support during a bull-market pullback is often more likely to hold.

Support during a bear-market decline is often more likely to fail.

Support within a leading sector may attract institutional buying.


                                               - 121 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 130 / 686>
Support within a weak sector may represent nothing more than a temporary pause.

Support and resistance should never be analyzed in isolation.



Case Review

The Transformation of Support and Resistance in Maison Verde

Now let's return to Maison Verde.

Why was it such an effective teaching example?

Because it demonstrated the complete evolution of support, resistance, and role reversal.

Stage One: Price rebounded several times around €38.

This suggested that buyers were actively supporting the area and that long-term investors might
also have considered the valuation attractive.

Stage Two: Price briefly broke below the lower boundary of the support zone but recovered
before the close, leaving behind a long lower shadow.

This showed that buyers responded aggressively beneath the support zone, preventing a
confirmed breakdown.

Stage Three: Price rallied toward €44.

That area had repeatedly capped previous advances, establishing a clear resistance zone.

Stage Four: Price broke above €44 on expanding volume and closed firmly above the resistance.

This indicated that buyers had begun absorbing the selling pressure overhead.

Stage Five: Price pulled back toward €44, trading volume contracted, and the stock never fell
back below the former resistance zone.

This suggested that the old resistance had begun transforming into new support.

That is the type of structural transition professional traders prefer to see.

If Ethan wanted to trade Maison Verde, the better entry would not have been the first touch of
€38, nor would it have been immediately after the breakout above €44.

The more disciplined opportunity would have been after the successful retest, when price held
above the breakout level while trading volume contracted.

At that point, risk becomes much easier to define.

If price falls back below €44, the breakout has failed.

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 131 / 686>
If price successfully defends €44 and resumes moving higher, the market has accepted the new
price area.

Trading is not about finding the cheapest price.

It is about finding the point where the evidence is strongest and the risk is most clearly defined.

Chapter Trading Rules

That day, I asked Ethan to write down five rules.

First: Support and resistance are zones, not precise price levels.

Second: Simply reaching a support zone does not mean it is time to buy. Always observe the
buyers' reaction first.

Third: Simply reaching a resistance zone does not mean price must decline. Watch whether the
breakout is supported by expanding volume.

Fourth: After a successful breakout, old resistance may become new support. After a confirmed
breakdown, old support may become new resistance.

Fifth: Support and resistance must always be evaluated together with trading volume, the broader
market environment, and subsequent confirmation.

Then I added one more sentence.

Support and resistance are not commands that the market must obey.

They are places where the market's memory becomes concentrated.

What truly matters is not that price has reached those areas.

What matters is how market participants behave after price gets there.

Ethan wrote that sentence into his notebook.

Later, I noticed that he had drawn a small arrow beneath it and written four simple words beside
it.

Watch the reaction.

Those four words may be more valuable than any complicated technical indicator.

He no longer viewed support as a level where price must rise.

Support and resistance are not permanent identities.

Professional analysis is not about asking whether price has reached a particular line.

It is about observing how the market reacts after price reaches an important area.

"What happened here before?"

"How has trading volume changed now that price has returned to this area?"

"If price breaks above or below this level, will the market accept the new price?"
