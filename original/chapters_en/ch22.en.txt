Five Questions Before You Buy: Real Trading Begins Before You Place the Order
Many investors believe that trading begins the moment they click the Buy button.

It doesn't.

Real trading begins long before you ever place an order.

The moment a particular stock first catches your attention, the trade has already begun.

The moment you draw your first Support and Resistance levels on the chart, the trade has already
begun.

The moment you start imagining how high the stock might rise, the trade has already begun.

And the moment you begin convincing yourself, "This looks like a good opportunity," your
decision-making has entered its most dangerous stage.

Because before you actually place an order, the market has not yet caused you any financial loss.

Your losses often begin in your mind first.

You first accept a vague reason for buying.

Then you tolerate an unclear entry point.

Next, you ignore the absence of a clearly defined stop-loss.

Finally, you end the entire decision-making process by telling yourself,

         "I'll just buy a small position and see what happens."

Many bad trades do not become bad after the purchase.

They were already destined to be difficult to manage before the order was ever placed.

Ethan Carter truly understood this lesson while studying a cybersecurity company.

For the purposes of this book, I will call it NordShield Cybersecurity.

NordShield Cybersecurity is a European enterprise cybersecurity software company specializing
in zero-trust access, cloud identity authentication, industrial network protection, and ransomware
monitoring. Its clients include financial institutions, manufacturing companies, healthcare
organizations, and government contractors.

At that time, cybersecurity was one of the market's hottest themes.

I. An Opportunity Is Not a Trade

NordShield's chart certainly looked attractive.

From a chart pattern perspective, it had formed a well-defined trading range near the highs.

From a Trading Volume perspective, selling pressure had gradually diminished throughout the
consolidation.

From a sector perspective, cybersecurity was clearly outperforming the broader market.

From a fundamental perspective, the company also had a reasonable long-term growth story.

But even when all these factors are combined, they tell us only one thing:

The stock deserves our attention.

They do not tell us:

That it should be bought immediately.

This is one of the most common mistakes investors make.

A Watchlist is not the same thing as a trade.

A stock may deserve extensive research without offering a valid entry.

A company may be outstanding, while its current price is not.

A chart pattern may look beautiful, yet still lack breakout confirmation.

A sector may be extremely strong, while the individual stock offers an unfavorable Risk-Reward
Ratio.

II. Five Questions Before You Buy

I asked Ethan to sit down and place his chart on the desk.

Then I wrote five questions on the whiteboard.

First: What is my reason for buying?

Second: What is my confirmation signal?

Third: If I'm wrong, where will the market prove that I'm wrong?

Fourth: What is the maximum amount I am willing to lose?

Then I told him,

        "Until you can answer all five questions clearly, you are not allowed to place the
        order."

Ethan looked at the whiteboard and frowned.

        "Isn't that a little too slow?"

        "Moving slowly is far better than trying to think after you've already lost money."

Most traders work in exactly the wrong order.

They buy first.

Then they search for reasons.

Then the stock declines.

Then they begin thinking about a stop-loss.

Then they discover that their position size is too large.

Finally, they start praying for a rebound.

Professional traders do the exact opposite.

First, define the reason.

Then define the confirmation.

Then define the invalidation point.

Then determine the position size.

Only after all of that do they place the order.

Trading is not a game of reaction.

It is a game of preparation.

I. The Basic Structure of a Breakout Trading Plan

A complete breakout trading plan should include at least seven components.

First: Market Environment

Does the overall market support new long positions?

Is the sector demonstrating Relative Strength?

Is overall market risk appetite stable?

Second: Trade Thesis

Does the company—or the ETF's investment theme—have reasonable fundamental support?

Or is the move simply driven by a single news event?

Third: Price Structure

Is the breakout emerging from a trading range, a Bull Flag, a bottoming pattern, or a previous
Resistance level?

Fourth: Confirmation Requirements

Will an intraday breakout be sufficient?

Or is a confirmed daily close required?

Must Trading Volume expand?

Should the sector confirm the move as well?

Fifth: Entry Method

Will you buy the breakout itself?

Or wait for a successful retest?



                                               - 496 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 505 / 686>
Will you enter the full position immediately?

Or build the position gradually?

Sixth: Risk Definition

What will you do if price falls back below the breakout level?

What if it closes back inside the trading range?

Where is your Stop-Loss?

What is your maximum acceptable loss?

Seventh: Exit Plan

Where is your first profit target?

Where is your second target?

What will you do if the advance begins losing momentum?

Will you reduce your position after reaching your target?

Without these elements, a breakout trade is nothing more than chasing price.

II. Three Breakout Entry Methods

There are three common ways to enter a breakout trade.

1. Enter Immediately on the Breakout

The advantage is that you are less likely to miss a powerful move.

The disadvantage is that it carries the highest risk of a False Breakout and makes it easy to buy at
the peak of market emotion.

This approach is suitable only for highly experienced traders with strong discipline and the ability
to execute stop-losses without hesitation.

2. Enter After the Closing Confirmation

The advantage is that it filters out some intraday false breakouts.

The disadvantage is that your entry price may be higher.

For most traders, this is the more appropriate approach.

3. Enter After a Successful Retest

The advantage is that the risk level becomes much clearer.



                                                - 497 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 506 / 686>
The disadvantage is that exceptionally strong trends may never provide a retest opportunity.

This method is better suited to more conservative investors.

III. Designing the Stop-Loss for a Breakout Trade

The most common mistake in breakout trading is focusing only on the profit target while ignoring
the stop-loss.

I wanted Ethan to remember one principle:

        The stop-loss for a breakout trade must always be tied to the logic behind the
        breakout.

If your reason for buying is a breakout above Resistance, then price falling back below that
resistance level becomes a warning signal.




                                              - 498 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 507 / 686>
If your reason for buying is a breakout from a trading range, then price returning to the range
signals that the breakout has failed.

If your reason for buying is a Bull Flag breakout, then price moving back inside the flag
invalidates the trade.

If your reason for buying is a Neckline Breakout, then price falling back below the Neckline
invalidates the setup.

A stop-loss should never be placed at an arbitrary percentage.

It belongs at the point where the trading thesis is proven wrong.

Of course, normal price volatility must also be considered.

If the stop-loss is placed too close, ordinary market fluctuations may trigger it prematurely.

If it is placed too far away, the potential loss becomes too large, requiring a smaller position size.

That is why Stop-Loss placement and Position Size should always be designed together.

IV. Setting Profit Targets for a Breakout Trade

Profit targets in breakout trading generally come from three sources.

First: Previous Resistance Areas

If there is an obvious overhead resistance zone or a previous high, it may serve as the first profit
target.

Second: Measured Move Targets

The height of a trading range, the length of a flagpole, or the size of a bottoming pattern can all be
used to estimate the potential upside.

Third: Trailing Exits

If the breakout develops into a strong trend, there is no need to sell the entire position
immediately.

Instead, the remaining position can be managed using the 20-Day Moving Average, the previous
swing low, or the prevailing trendline.

A profit target is not a prediction of the future.

Its purpose is to help you determine:

When to reduce your position.

When to protect your profits.

And when the Risk-Reward Ratio is no longer favorable.

Trading Rules for This Chapter

That day, I asked Ethan to write down seven rules.

Rule 1: An opportunity worth watching is not automatically a trade.

Rule 2: Before placing any order, you must answer five questions: your reason for buying, your
confirmation signal, your invalidation point, your maximum acceptable loss, and your exit plan.

Rule 3: In breakout trading, intraday price action alone is not enough. A confirmed closing
breakout carries far greater significance.

Rule 4: Every breakout must be supported by Trading Volume. Without volume confirmation,
always remain alert for a False Breakout.
