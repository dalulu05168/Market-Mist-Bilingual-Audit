The Candlestick That Chased the Price: The Trap Beginners Fall Into Most Easily
By Ethan Carter's second week in the Research Department, the market had prepared a far more
aggressive lesson for him.

During his first week, he had still been adjusting to the rhythm of a professional research
institution.

Every morning before eight o'clock, he arrived at the office, logged into his terminal, reviewed
the overnight U.S. market, Asian markets, European futures, Treasury yields, crude oil prices, the
U.S. Dollar Index, and the performance of the major sector ETFs. He then organized what he
considered the most important information into his notebook, hoping to form what he believed
was a "well-founded opinion" before the morning meeting began.

Young research analysts are usually hardworking.

But hard work alone does not guarantee sound judgment.

If a person's analytical framework is fundamentally flawed, the harder they work, the further they
may push themselves toward the wrong conclusion.

At the time, Ethan was in exactly that stage.

He had begun to understand that price alone was not enough.

But he had not yet truly learned how to wait for confirmation.

He knew that trading volume mattered.

But he still could not distinguish whether expanding volume represented genuine buying interest
or merely crowded trading activity.

He understood that a resistance level was not some mysterious line.

Yet whenever price broke through it rapidly, his heartbeat still accelerated.

It is a problem shared by many investors.

It is not that they know nothing.

The problem is that their knowledge has not yet become discipline.

And the market enjoys exploiting the gap between the two more than anything else.

It was a Thursday.

Shortly after the European market opened, a renewable energy equipment company suddenly
appeared on every screen in the Research Department.

I. Before You Chase the Price, the Market Has Already Scripted Your Emotions

At 10:03 a.m., the Research Department called an unscheduled team discussion.

The renewable energy sector had suddenly become unusually active, and the industry team
needed to determine whether capital was rotating out of defensive sectors and back into growth
stocks.

Ethan was asked to present a brief assessment.

Standing beside the conference table, with HelioGrid's chart displayed on the screen, he began,

"From a technical perspective, HelioGrid has broken above the upper boundary of a two-month
trading range with significantly expanding trading volume. At the sector level, the Energy
Storage ETF is also moving higher today. I believe this could be an early indication that capital is
returning to renewable energy equipment stocks."

His presentation was noticeably more mature than it had been during his first week.

At least he was no longer saying only,

"The price has gone up."

He had begun incorporating sector strength, trading volume, and market structure into his
analysis.

But I still heard one dangerous phrase.

Could be.

The market is full of possibilities.

But trades cannot be built on possibilities alone.

A trade requires conditions.

It requires confirmation.

It requires an exit plan.

I asked him,

"Do you believe this is a confirmed breakout, or merely a breakout attempt?"

Ethan hesitated.

"At the moment... it looks like a confirmed breakout."

"Why?"

"Because trading volume is very high."

"Does high volume automatically make it a confirmed breakout?"

The conference room fell silent.

I walked to the screen and pulled up the intraday transaction data.

"Look here."

"Price is trading aggressively above 42.50."

"But after every push higher, the sell orders above become noticeably heavier."

"Buyers are certainly active."

"But sellers are equally active."

"In other words, this is not one-sided buying."

"It is a large transfer of shares taking place near the highs."

Many investors assume that expanding volume automatically means institutions are buying.

But they forget one fundamental fact.

Expanding volume simply tells us that disagreement is increasing.

It does not tell us who has won.

If volume expands, price continues advancing, and later holds above the breakout level during a
pullback, then buyers have gained control.

If volume expands but price stalls, retreats, or even falls back into the previous trading range,
sellers may simply be using the increased liquidity to distribute shares.

I looked at Ethan.

"Right now, you've seen the breakout."

"But you haven't yet seen acceptance."

He nodded.

Yet only a few minutes later, he glanced back at the screen again.

The price had already climbed to 44.

Sometimes the market teaches people in remarkably cruel ways.

It rarely proves you right while you're still hesitating.

...before tempting you into making a decision at a far worse price.

That was exactly the psychological pressure Ethan was experiencing.

If he bought now, he felt he was acting impulsively.

If he didn't buy, he feared he might miss an entire trend.

This is the classic state of FOMO.

You are no longer buying because you understand the opportunity.

You are buying because you cannot tolerate watching everyone else make money while you

II. Ethan's First Paper Trade

The research institution provided every new research analyst with a paper trading account.

The account had no impact on real capital, but every trade required a written explanation of the
trade thesis, entry price, stop-loss level, profit target, and post-trade review.

Its purpose was not to prove that new analysts could make money.

Its purpose was to expose their trading habits.

That afternoon, Ethan finally submitted his first paper trade request.

Buy HelioGrid.

Entry Price: 44.20

Trade Thesis: Breakout above a two-month trading range, expanding trading volume, and sector-
wide strength.

Stop Loss: 41.80

Target Price: 48.00

Position Size: 15% of the paper trading account.

I read through it but did not approve it immediately.

"Why 44.20?"

"Because it continued moving higher after the breakout."

"Why not around 42.50?"

"At that point, the breakout hadn't been confirmed yet."

"And has it been confirmed now?"

Ethan paused.

"The price has already moved away from the breakout level. That suggests the buying pressure is
strong."

"Or it may simply suggest that you've chased it too late."

His expression stiffened.

It wasn't an easy thing to hear.

Professor's Whiteboard Session

False Breakout / Bull Trap Pattern

That evening, I taught Ethan what would later become one of the most valuable technical lessons
of his career:

False Breakouts and Bull Traps.




                                             - 38 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 47 / 686>
Many investors like to trade breakouts.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that.

Breakout trading is one of the most important trading approaches because when price breaks
above a major resistance level, it often signals that the market's previous balance has been
disrupted.

If the breakout is genuine, a new trend may begin from that point.

The problem is this:

Not every breakout is a real breakout.

The market is filled with situations in which price briefly pushes above a key level, attracts
momentum buyers, and then quickly falls back into the previous trading range.

That is a False Breakout.

When a false breakout occurs on the upside, draws investors into buying at higher prices, and is
followed by a sharp decline, we generally call it a:

Bull Trap.



I. How a Bull Trap Forms

A classic Bull Trap typically develops in five stages.

Stage One: Price Remains Below Resistance for an Extended Period

Price repeatedly attempts to break through the same resistance area but fails.

Gradually, that resistance level becomes obvious to the entire market.

The more investors who are watching the same resistance level, the more attention the breakout is
likely to attract once it occurs.

Stage Two: Price Suddenly Breaks Above Resistance

The breakout may be driven by news.

It may be supported by sector-wide strength.

Or it may simply be fueled by short-term speculative buying.

On the breakout day, price accelerates rapidly.

Momentum traders begin to get excited.




                                               - 39 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 48 / 686>
Stage Three: Trading Volume Expands Sharply

The surge in volume makes the breakout appear far more convincing.

But expanding volume alone does not prove that buyers have won.

It merely tells us that a large number of shares changed hands at that level.

If price cannot continue advancing, heavy volume may instead indicate that significant selling
pressure is waiting overhead.

Stage Four: Price Falls Back Below Resistance

This is the critical stage.

A genuine breakout should remain above the former resistance level whenever possible.

If price quickly falls back beneath it, the market is telling you that it has not accepted the new
price created by the breakout.

Stage Five: Momentum Buyers Become Trapped

Investors who bought near the breakout highs begin taking losses.

If they placed stop losses, those stops are triggered once price falls below the key level.

If they refuse to stop out, they become trapped at higher prices...

...creating additional selling pressure during future rebounds.

II. Why Beginners Find It So Easy to Chase Price

False Breakouts occur over and over again because they exploit one thing above all else:

Human nature.

Human nature dislikes waiting.

When price moves sideways, most people become bored.

When a stock quietly consolidates near the lows, most people believe there is no opportunity.

When a new trend is just beginning, most people remain skeptical.

Only after the price has already advanced do most people finally become convinced.

The problem is that by the time the majority finally believes...


                                               - 41 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 50 / 686>
...the price is usually no longer cheap.

That was exactly what happened to Ethan.

When HelioGrid was breaking out around 42.50, he felt there was not enough confirmation.

By the time the price reached 44.20, he believed the strength had become obvious.

But from a trading perspective, 44.20 was no safer than 42.50.

It simply felt safer.

That is one of the most subtle aspects of market psychology.

The truly attractive entry points usually make people uncomfortable.

The entry points that make people feel comfortable are often no longer attractive.

In the early stages of a breakout, uncertainty is still high.

That uncertainty keeps prices relatively low.

Near the highs, confidence appears much greater.

And that confidence comes with a higher price.

Many investors end up paying a premium simply to purchase emotional comfort.



III. How to Determine Whether a Breakout Is Reliable

I wrote four conditions on the whiteboard for Ethan.

First, Examine the Breakout Level

The more significant the resistance level being broken, the more confirmation is required.

If price is breaking above a major long-term resistance level, one breakout candlestick alone is
never enough to declare that a new trend has begun.

The more market participants watching the same level...

...the greater the probability of a False Breakout.

Second, Evaluate the Nature of the Trading Volume

Expanding volume during a breakout is generally positive.

But what matters even more is how price responds after that volume appears.

If volume expands and price continues advancing, buyers are likely in control.



                                                 - 42 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 51 / 686>
If volume expands but price stalls, selling pressure may be just as strong.

If heavy volume produces a candlestick with a long upper shadow, investors should become
especially cautious.

Third, Observe the Closing Price

An intraday breakout is not the same as a confirmed breakout.

Many False Breakouts temporarily move above resistance during the trading session...

...only to close back below it before the market closes.

For daily chart traders, the closing price is often far more important than the highest intraday
price.

Fourth, Watch the Retest

The pullback after a breakout is one of the most important confirmation processes.

A healthy retest generally has two characteristics.

First, price remains above the former resistance level.

Second, trading volume contracts noticeably during the pullback.

If the retest breaks back below the breakout level on expanding volume, the probability of a failed
breakout rises substantially.

IV. What Happens After a False Breakout Fails?

Once a False Breakout fails, the market typically follows one of three paths.

Scenario One: Price Returns to the Trading Range and Continues Consolidating

This is the mildest outcome.

After the failed breakout, price simply moves back into the previous range without experiencing a
significant decline.

This suggests that buyers and sellers are still competing for control and that no meaningful trend
has yet emerged.

Scenario Two: Price Falls Back Into the Range and Tests Lower Support

This situation is considerably more dangerous.



                                               - 44 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 53 / 686>
Investors who chased the breakout are now trapped.

As excitement turns into disappointment, many begin selling to limit their losses.

That selling creates additional downward pressure on the market.

Scenario Three: The False Breakout Becomes the Beginning of a Trend Reversal

Sometimes a failed breakout marks the start of an entirely new trend in the opposite direction.

This is especially common near major market highs.

When a breakout fails and is accompanied by heavy volume, a long upper shadow, or a decisive
break below an important support level, it may indicate that institutional investors have used the
breakout to distribute shares.

That is precisely why I repeatedly reminded Ethan:

"Don't focus only on where price breaks through."

"Focus on what happens after it breaks through."

"Does the market actually accept the new price?"

On the surface, Ethan's reasoning was not entirely wrong.

He correctly recognized the sector strength.

He correctly identified the breakout.

He correctly noticed the surge in trading volume.

The problem was that he overlooked three critical points.

First, he failed to wait for confirmation at the close.

Second, his entry price was already too far above the breakout level.

Third, he immediately interpreted expanding volume as strong buying pressure without observing

What would the proper trading plan have looked like?

If Ethan wanted to trade the breakout, he had two more disciplined choices.

The first would have been to initiate a small exploratory position near the breakout while placing

failed.

The second would have been to wait for the post-breakout pullback and observe whether price
found support around 42.50.

If the pullback occurred on declining volume and price resumed moving higher, then he could
consider entering.

Both approaches were far more rational than chasing the stock at 44.20.

Because they were based on market structure...

...not on emotion.

Second: Expanding trading volume does not automatically mean buyers have won. Always
observe whether price continues to advance afterward.

Third: If your reason for buying is "I'm afraid of missing out," then you do not have a trading plan.

Chapter Summary

Ethan Carter's first paper trade did not cost him any real money.

But it revealed the most dangerous part of his own psychology:

The Fear of Missing Out.

Many mistakes in the market are not made because investors know nothing about technical
analysis.

They are made because investors know just enough to act...

...but not enough to wait.

They know that breakouts matter.

So they chase every breakout.

They know that trading volume matters.

So they buy whenever volume expands.

They know that hot sectors tend to attract capital.

So they become excited every time positive news appears.

Professional investors understand something different.

Technical signals require confirmation.
