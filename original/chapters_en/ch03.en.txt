Chapter 3
Mr. Market's Temper: Why the Market Always Moves Against Human Nature
After Chapter 2, Ethan Carter's pace of trading began to slow down.

That was a good thing.

For many new research analysts entering the market, the most important skill they need to
develop is not making decisions more quickly, but acting more slowly.

The market creates the illusion that good opportunities disappear in an instant, making you
believe that you must react immediately.

But truly mature investors understand that while the market fluctuates every day, worthwhile
trading opportunities do not appear every day.

Before placing an order, Ethan began asking himself a few additional questions.

Has the breakout been confirmed?

Has trading volume confirmed the move?

Has the price completed a retest?

Where is my stop loss?

Am I analyzing the market, or am I simply afraid of missing out?

These questions helped him make fewer mistakes.

But the market soon found another way to test him.

If the lesson in Chapter 2 came from a rising market, then the lesson in Chapter 3 would come
from a falling one.

It was a Tuesday morning.

The European market opened steadily.

The major indices posted modest gains.

Banking stocks remained stable.

Technology stocks rebounded slightly.

Bond yields showed no significant change.

There was no major negative news in the headlines.

The atmosphere inside the Research Department was even somewhat relaxed.

Sitting at his desk, Ethan was organizing a short report on the consumer sector. Compared with
the previous few days, he had become much quieter. Occasionally, he looked up at the index
chart, but he spent most of his time reading corporate announcements and industry data.

At 10:27 a.m., the market suddenly began to decline.

At first, only a few heavyweight stocks pulled back.

Then the index quickly erased its 0.4% gain and turned negative.

Soon afterward, the loss widened to 0.8%.

Fifteen minutes later, the major European indices broke below the short-term support that had
held over the previous five trading sessions.

The atmosphere on the trading desk changed immediately.

Several traders began typing rapidly on their keyboards.

The macro team started checking bond yields and the euro exchange rate.

The news terminals refreshed continuously, yet no major headline appeared that could adequately
explain the decline.

Ethan looked up, frowned, and asked,

"What happened?"

That is often the first reaction investors have when the market suddenly declines.

They assume that every price movement must immediately have a corresponding news
explanation.

But the market does not always work that way.

Very often, price moves first, and the news arrives later.

Very often, the news merely provides an explanation rather than the true cause.

Very often, what truly drives price is not the facts themselves, but the market's repricing of future
expectations.

Ethan opened the news terminal and quickly searched for keywords.

No major negative news.

No central bank speech.

No escalation of war.

No disastrous earnings report.

No sudden policy change.

He became even more confused.

"There isn't any bad news. Why is the market falling so much?"

I did not answer immediately.

Instead, I walked over to his screen and enlarged the daily chart of the index.

"Look here."

"For the past two weeks, the index has been trading sideways within a narrow range. The support

"But today, the index broke below that support."

"And it didn't break it slowly."

"It broke it quickly."

I turned to Ethan.

"If many people regard this level as support, what happens after it breaks?"

He thought for a moment.

"Stop-loss orders will be triggered."

"And what else?"

"People who were planning to buy will hesitate."

"And what else?"

"Short sellers may enter the market."

I nodded.

"That is why the market suddenly accelerates. Not because the news suddenly becomes worse,
but because the market structure has been broken."

I. Why Does Price Move Ahead of the News?

Ethan was still staring at the news terminal.

"But there has to be a reason, doesn't there?"

"Of course there is," I replied. "It's just that the reason doesn't necessarily appear in the form of a
news headline."

This is something I often remind the research analysts.

The financial markets are not a news-reading exam.

If you trade solely based on publicly available news, you are usually already standing near the
back of the line. By the time a headline appears on your screen, much of the capital may have

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 61 / 686>
already acted in advance based on expectations, economic data, order flow, positioning, and risk
appetite.

I asked Ethan,

"What do you think the market is actually trading?"

"Information."

"More precisely, expectations."

Information is merely the raw material.

Expectations are the true driving force behind price movement.

The very same piece of news can produce completely different market reactions under different
expectations.

If market expectations are already extremely pessimistic, even an ordinary piece of news may be
interpreted as bullish.

If market expectations are already very optimistic, even good news may trigger a decline.

If the market has already priced in positive news beforehand, the day that news is officially
announced may instead become a selling opportunity.

Ethan nodded.

But he had not yet fully connected that logic with the decline unfolding before him.

I continued,

"Today's decline may not yet have a news explanation. But structurally, it already has an
explanation."

I pointed to the support level on the chart.

"Over the past two weeks, many market participants have treated this level as a short-term
defensive line. As price repeatedly rebounded from this area, bullish traders became increasingly
convinced that the support would hold. They bought around this level and placed their stop losses
just below it. Short sellers were watching the same level, waiting for a breakdown to enter. Now
that support has failed, bullish stop losses are being triggered, short sellers are adding positions,
and sidelined capital is stepping aside. These three forces are pushing the market lower
simultaneously."

Ethan said quietly,

"So it wasn't the news that caused the decline. It was the change in behavior after support was
broken."

"Exactly."

That is the importance of price structure.

A support level is not some mysterious number.

It is a focal point of market behavior.

When support holds, buyers are willing to step in, short sellers hesitate to press lower, and price is
more likely to rebound.

When support fails, buyers become sellers, short sellers become more aggressive, and what was
once a safe zone becomes a resistance area.

The market does not decline because of a line on the chart.

The market declines because market participants change their behavior around that line.



II. Ethan's Second Mistake: Searching for News Instead of Observing Structure

By 11:00 a.m., the index had extended its decline to 1.3%.

Ethan finally stopped searching the news.

He switched the chart to the fifteen-minute timeframe and began observing the rebound after the
support breakdown.

Price briefly rebounded toward the former support level but quickly met resistance.

I asked,

"If you were a short-term trader right now, how would you interpret this?"

Ethan replied,

"If the rebound can't reclaim the support level, then that support may have turned into resistance."

"Very good."

"That would suggest the decline may not be over yet."

"Go on."

"If the rebound occurs on declining volume, it suggests buying interest is insufficient. If selling
volume expands again afterward, it may confirm continued weakness."

I nodded.

He was beginning to shift from news-based thinking to structure-based thinking.

That was progress.

But the market rarely rewards someone immediately after they begin making progress.

At 11:30 a.m., the index started falling again from the rebound.

This time, the decline was even faster.

Several heavyweight stocks broke below key levels simultaneously, and the sector ETFs also
began to weaken.

What had initially been only a short-term support breakdown in the index was gradually evolving
into a broader deterioration in market risk appetite.

Looking at the chart, Ethan asked,

"Should we short the market now?"

I smiled.

"You see? The market has just taught you to stop chasing rallies, and now you want to chase the
decline."

He looked a little embarrassed.

This is another common extreme among beginners.

When prices are rising, they fear missing the rally.

When prices are falling, they fear missing the decline.

Fundamentally, they are still allowing price to dictate their decisions.

I said,

"You may conclude that the market is weak, but that doesn't mean every price level is suitable for
initiating a short position. Short selling, just like buying, requires a plan."

"So what should I wait for?"

"Wait for the rebound to fail, or wait for a new market structure to develop."

He looked at the rebound on the chart.

"So... wait for confirmation."

"Exactly. Many mistakes in the market come from the same cause: acting before confirmation."

Professor's Whiteboard Session

Support Breakdown and Retest

That afternoon, the topic of Ethan's whiteboard lesson was:

Support Breakdown and Retest.

This is one of the most fundamental yet most important concepts in technical analysis.

Many investors know how to draw support levels but have no idea how to interpret the market
once support fails.

They often assume,

"If price reaches support, it's time to buy."



                                                - 56 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 65 / 686>
But that is not how professionals evaluate the market.

Before it is broken, a support level may indeed serve as a buying area.

Once it is decisively broken, however, it may become an area of risk.

I. Why Does Support Form?

Support levels generally originate from three types of market memory.

First: Cost Memory

Many investors previously bought shares within this price area.

When price returns to this level, they believe it represents good value and are willing to buy again
or add to their positions.

Second: Technical Memory

A large number of traders are watching the same swing low, the same horizontal support line, or
the lower boundary of the same trading range.

As price approaches this level, technical buying interest begins to emerge.

Third: Psychological Memory

Round-number price levels, previous swing lows, and major long-term moving averages often
become psychological defensive lines for investors.

A support level is not effective because it possesses any inherent power.

It is effective because a large number of market participants believe it has power and act
accordingly.



II. Why Does Support Fail?

A support failure usually indicates that the buying interest which previously defended that level is
no longer sufficient.

It may be because market expectations have deteriorated.

It may be because institutional investors are no longer supporting the market.

It may be because concerns about fundamentals have begun to emerge.

It may be because overall risk appetite has weakened.

Or it may simply be because too many traders placed their stop-loss orders at the same level,
triggering a chain reaction of selling once support was broken.



                                               - 57 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 66 / 686>
Once support fails, market behavior changes rapidly.

Those who originally bought at support begin taking losses.

Those who had been waiting on the sidelines hesitate to enter.

Those looking to sell short recognize an opportunity.

Those hoping to buy the dip begin questioning their assumptions.

As a result, an accelerated decline often develops below a broken support level.

This is not the magic of lines drawn on a chart.

It is the result of changing market behavior.



III. Why Is the Retest So Important?

After a support level is broken, price will often stage a rebound.

The purpose of that rebound is to test the market's attitude.

If price quickly recovers above the broken support level, the breakdown may prove to be a false
breakdown.

If price rebounds toward the former support but fails to reclaim it, the previous support has now
become new resistance.

At that point, the market provides much stronger confirmation that weakness remains.

Therefore, the key after a support breakdown is not to act immediately.

The key is to observe the retest.

A failed retest is often far more meaningful than the initial breakdown itself.

Case Review

The Index Didn't Fall Without a Reason—The Structure Changed First

Now let's return to the index movement that day.

If Ethan had focused only on the news, he would have remained confused.

"There isn't any bad news. Why is the market falling?"

But if he had focused on market structure, the answer would have been obvious.

First, the index had been trading sideways within a narrow range for several days.

Second, the support level below had become the focus of many market participants.

Third, once support was broken, stop-loss orders were triggered while short sellers entered the
market simultaneously.


                                              - 59 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 68 / 686>
Fourth, the rebound failed to reclaim the broken support.

Fifth, the former support became new resistance, confirming the decline.

That is what a structural decline looks like.

Of course, explanations eventually appeared in the news afterward.

Perhaps an analyst claimed that the market was worried about slowing economic growth.

Perhaps the media reported that investors were taking profits.

Perhaps commentators suggested that investors were waiting for the upcoming central bank
meeting.

But all of those explanations came after the fact.

The real clues had already appeared earlier in the market's price structure.



Chapter Trading Rules

That day, I asked Ethan to write down three rules.

First: Never rush to explain the market with news when there is no structural evidence.

Second: Once a support level has been broken, do not immediately assume it will continue to
hold.

Third: A failed retest often provides stronger confirmation of weakness than the initial breakdown
itself.

Then I added one more sentence.

The market does not always tell you the reason before it begins to move.

Very often, the price structure itself is already part of the reason.



Chapter Summary

What Ethan Carter learned in this chapter was not how to predict every market decline.

It was how to stop panicking and searching for news after a decline had already begun.

He gradually came to understand:

There is nothing unusual about price moving first.

There is nothing unusual about the news arriving later.
