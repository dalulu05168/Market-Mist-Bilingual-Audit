Chapter 23
Stop-Loss Is Not Defeat: It Is an Investor's Seat Belt
Many investors claim they always use Stop-Losses.

But when the moment finally arrives to execute one, they suddenly become remarkably patient.

Before entering a trade, they say:

        "If it breaks below this level, I'll get out."

After buying, when price actually breaks below that level, they begin saying:

        "Let's wait a little longer. It could just be a False Breakdown."

        "The overall market is weak today. That doesn't count."

        "The company's fundamentals haven't changed."

        "I'm investing for the long term anyway."

        "It's only a small loss. It'll recover eventually."

As a result, what could have remained a small, controlled loss gradually turns into a devastating
one.

The most expensive mistake in the market is not being wrong.

Being wrong is perfectly normal.

The most expensive mistake is refusing to admit you are wrong.

Ethan Carter did not truly learn how to use a Stop-Loss during his biggest losing trade.

Quite the opposite.

He learned it during a trade that lost only a very small amount.

There was nothing dramatic about that trade.

Nothing exciting.

It made no profit.

Yet it taught him something for the first time.

A Stop-Loss is not failure.

A Stop-Loss is what allows you to stay in the game long enough to participate in the next
opportunity.

I. A Trade That Looked Almost Perfect

in enterprise cloud hosting, hybrid cloud deployment, data backup security, and edge computing
solutions.

It was not the market's most glamorous cloud computing company, but it had a stable business
model. Most of its clients were medium-sized and large enterprises, and a significant portion of
its revenue came from long-term contracts and subscription services.

At that time, institutional capital had begun returning to the cloud infrastructure sector.

There were three primary reasons.

Corporate IT spending was increasing again.

Artificial intelligence applications were driving demand for computing power and data storage.

And after a prolonged correction, valuations across several cloud service providers had become

The chart of Vega Cloud was equally attractive.

The 20-Day Moving Average flattened out.

        "How can I tell the difference between a normal pullback and a failed pattern?"

Every trader must answer that question.

Because if your Stop-Loss is too loose, your losses become unnecessarily large.

If it is too tight, ordinary market fluctuations will force you out of perfectly good trades.

I. What Is a Failed Pattern?

A failed pattern occurs when price fails to move in the direction implied by the chart pattern and
instead returns to—or breaks through—the pattern's critical price levels, invalidating the original
trading thesis.

Common examples include:

       A breakout from a trading range that falls back into the range.
       A breakout above a Neckline that fails and drops back below it.
       A Bull Flag Breakout that falls back inside the flag.
       A Double Bottom Breakout that breaks back below the Neckline.
       An Inverse Head and Shoulders breakout that falls back below the Neckline.
       A Double Top Breakdown that quickly recovers back above the Neckline.

A failed pattern is not an ordinary price fluctuation.

It is the invalidation of a critical Market Structure.

Therefore, before entering any trade, you must already know:

Where is the confirmation level?

Where is the invalidation level?

Which movements are merely normal price fluctuations?

And at what point must you exit?

II. Three Types of Stop-Losses

Stop-losses can generally be divided into three categories.

1. Structural Stop-Loss

This is the most important type of stop-loss.

It is based on Market Structure.

For example:

       Breaking below Support.
       Falling back into a trading range.
       Breaking below a Neckline.


                                                - 520 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 529 / 686>
       Falling back inside a Bull Flag.
       Breaking below the previous swing low.

The greatest advantage of a structural stop-loss is that it is directly tied to your original trading
thesis.

2. Account Risk Stop-Loss

This type of stop-loss is determined by your overall account risk.

For example, limiting the maximum loss on any single trade to 0.5%, 1%, or 2% of total account
equity.

Its purpose is to prevent one trade from causing significant damage to your account.

3. Time Stop-Loss

Sometimes a trade never reaches the stop-loss level, yet it still fails to perform as expected.

If, after a breakout, price fails to continue higher for an extended period, Trading Volume
continues declining, the sector weakens, and capital efficiency deteriorates, it may be appropriate
to exit the trade.

A Time Stop-Loss is designed for trades that have not lost much money—but also have not
developed according to the original plan.

III. Why Must the Stop-Loss Be Written Before You Enter the Trade?

A Stop-Loss must be defined before you enter a trade.

Never afterward.

The reason is simple.

Before entering a position, your judgment is relatively objective.

After entering the trade, your position begins influencing your thinking.

Owning the stock changes the way your brain processes information.

You become more willing to believe favorable news.

You become more likely to ignore warning signs.

You begin interpreting ordinary rebounds as hope.

You explain obvious breakdowns as temporary shakeouts.

That is why your Stop-Loss must be written in advance.

Writing it down will not eliminate emotion completely.

But when emotions inevitably appear, it gives you an objective standard to return to.

I told Ethan,

        "A Stop-Loss is not a tool you need only after the market starts falling. It is the
        seat belt you fasten before you begin the journey."

No one waits until after a car crashes into a wall before putting on a seat belt.

V. Reviewing a Trade After a Stop-Loss

A Stop-Loss is not the end of the trading process.

After every stop-loss, you should conduct a review.

But a review is not about blaming yourself.

It should answer five questions.

First: Was the original trading thesis reasonable?

Second: Was the confirmation signal sufficient?

Third: Was the Stop-Loss placed appropriately?

Fourth: Did you execute the trade according to your plan?

Fifth: Should any trading rules be improved before the next trade?

If the trade thesis was reasonable, the stop-loss was appropriate, and execution followed the plan,
then the loss was a qualified loss.

A qualified loss is not something to regret.

It is simply part of a properly functioning trading system.

The losses that deserve serious attention are unqualified losses.

For example:

       Entering a trade without a plan.
       Chasing price after it has already become extended.

       Delaying the exit after the invalidation level has already been broken.
       Turning a short-term trade into a long-term investment simply because it is losing money.

Losses themselves are not frightening.

Uncontrolled losses are.
