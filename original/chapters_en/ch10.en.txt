Chapter 10
The Trap of Chart Patterns: They May Look the Same, Yet Lead to Completely
Different Outcomes


The first time Ethan Carter was truly deceived by a chart pattern was because of a chart that
looked almost too perfect.

It looked as though it had been copied directly from a textbook.

A prolonged downtrend.

One low.

A rebound.

A second decline.

Then, instead of breaking sharply to new lows, price found buying support near the bottom.

With a little imagination, it even resembled a Head and Shoulders Bottom that was beginning to
take shape.

Ethan was genuinely excited.

It was not the first time he had seen a chart pattern.

But it was the first time he believed he had identified a major opportunity before everyone else.

This is exactly where many investors make their first serious mistake.

After learning about the Double Bottom, Triple Bottom, Head and Shoulders Bottom, Bull Flag,
and Double Top, the world of charts suddenly becomes filled with patterns.

What was once an ordinary consolidation now looks like a Double Bottom.

What was once a failed rebound now looks like a Head and Shoulders Bottom.

What was once sideways trading near the highs now resembles a Bull Flag.

What was once three failed attempts to break higher now appears to be a Triple Top.

During the early stages of learning chart patterns, the greatest danger is not failing to recognize
them.

It is seeing patterns everywhere.

The stock in question was one I will call Baltic Renewables.

Ethan became interested in it because the stock had been falling for a very long time.

That is also what attracts most beginners.

The more a stock falls, the cheaper it appears.

The longer it declines, the closer it seems to a bottom.

The more it rebounds, the more it looks like a reversal.

I. Looking Like a Pattern Does Not Mean the Pattern Exists

I asked Ethan to project the chart of Baltic Renewables onto the meeting room's large screen.

The chart was undeniably attractive.

The downtrend had lasted for a long time.

Price no longer seemed to be falling relentlessly.

The middle low was lower than both sides.

The low on the right was, for the moment, holding above the head.

Everything suggested that selling pressure might be weakening.

If you desperately wanted to see a Head and Shoulders Bottom, you certainly could.

But the market does not recognize a pattern simply because you recognize its shape.

The defining feature of a genuine Head and Shoulders Bottom is not how closely the left shoulder,
head, and right shoulder resemble the textbook illustration.

The real test is whether price can break above the Neckline, and whether Trading Volume
confirms that breakout.

Until price breaks above the Neckline, it is nothing more than a Potential Head and Shoulders
Bottom.

If price turns lower from the right shoulder and breaks below the low of the head, the entire
pattern fails.

I wrote on the whiteboard:

        Potential Pattern ≠ Confirmed Pattern

A Potential Pattern is not a Confirmed Pattern.

"You need to understand that the bottom is usually the point of greatest uncertainty. A confirmed

That is a difficult truth for many investors to accept.

They want to buy cheaply.

They also want complete safety.

And they want the stock to rise immediately.

The market rarely offers all three at once.

Buying before a pattern is confirmed may provide a lower entry price, but it also carries a much
higher probability of failure.

Buying after the breakout has been confirmed may result in a higher entry price, but the evidence
supporting the trend is much stronger.

Buying after the breakout and a successful retest may require paying an even higher price, but the
level of risk becomes much easier to define.

Every entry point comes with a different balance between risk and reward.

The real question is not which entry is always best.

The real question is whether you fully understand which risk you are choosing to accept.

Professor's Whiteboard Session

Potential Pattern vs Confirmed Pattern

That evening, the topic of my whiteboard session with Ethan was:

Potential Patterns and Confirmed Patterns.

Chart patterns are extremely useful tools.

But they also come with a natural danger.




                                             - 198 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 207 / 686>
The human brain is wired to recognize patterns.

We see animals in clouds.

We see faces in the texture of a wall.

And we see the patterns we want to see in price movements.

That is not stupidity.

It is simply how the human brain works.

In the market, however, that instinct often causes investors to reach conclusions too early.



I. What Is a Potential Pattern?

A Potential Pattern is a chart formation in which the basic structure has begun to appear, but the
key confirmation conditions have not yet been completed.

For example:

       A Double Bottom has formed two lows, but price has not yet broken above the Neckline.
       A Triple Bottom has formed three lows, but Trading Volume has not confirmed the
        pattern, and price has not broken above the Neckline.
       A Head and Shoulders Bottom has formed a left shoulder, a head, and a right shoulder,
        but price has not yet broken above the Neckline on expanding Trading Volume.
       A Bull Flag has completed its consolidation channel, but price has not yet broken above
        the upper boundary of the flag.
       A Double Top has formed two highs, but price has not yet broken below the Neckline.

All of these are merely Potential Patterns.

A Potential Pattern can be monitored.

It can be analyzed in advance.

It can be included in a trading plan.

But it must not be treated as a confirmed trading signal.



II. What Is a Confirmed Pattern?

A Confirmed Pattern is one in which price has completed the key breakout or breakdown, and
subsequent price action supports the validity of the pattern.

For bottom reversal patterns, confirmation generally requires:


                                              - 199 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 208 / 686>
        Price breaks above the Neckline or another key resistance level.
        Trading Volume expands during the breakout.
        Price closes firmly above the key level.
        Trading Volume contracts during the retest.
        The Neckline holds as support during the retest.
        Price resumes moving higher afterward.

For top reversal patterns, confirmation generally requires:

        Price breaks below the Neckline or another key support level.
        Trading Volume expands during the breakdown.
        The rebound fails to reclaim the former support level.
        Price continues weakening afterward.

For trend continuation patterns, confirmation generally requires:

        Trading Volume contracts during the consolidation.
        The breakout occurs in the direction of the existing trend.
        Trading Volume expands during the breakout.
        Price does not quickly fall back into the consolidation range after the breakout.

Confirmation is not a single event.

It is a process.

III. Why Must Every Pattern Wait for Confirmation?

There are three reasons why confirmation is essential.

First, It Filters Out Imagination

Many chart patterns continue evolving while they are still forming.

A Potential Double Bottom may eventually become a Triple Bottom.

A Potential Head and Shoulders Bottom may fail completely and continue moving lower.

What appears to be a Bull Flag may break out in the opposite direction.


                                             - 201 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 210 / 686>
What looks like a Double Top may instead break upward and become a continuation pattern.

If you reach conclusions too early, the market will often prove you wrong.

Second, It Confirms Which Side Has Gained Control

A chart pattern is only a structure.

The breakout is what reveals which side has actually won the battle.

Before the breakout, buyers and sellers are still competing.

Only after the breakout does the market begin to reveal who has taken control.

When Trading Volume expands together with the breakout, it indicates that institutional capital is
supporting that move.

Third, It Defines Risk

A confirmed pattern makes it much easier to define a logical stop-loss.

After a bottom breakout, if the retest falls back below the Neckline, the pattern may have failed.

After a top breakdown, if the rebound climbs back above the Neckline, the bearish pattern may
have failed.

After a flag breakout, if price falls back inside the flag, the continuation pattern may have failed.

Every trade must define where it is wrong.

If you do not know where you are wrong,

then you do not have a trading plan.



IV. Is It Acceptable to Buy Early?

Ethan once asked me a very practical question.

"If waiting for confirmation means giving up part of the profit, can I buy while the pattern is still
developing?"

The answer is:

Yes.

But you must understand exactly what you are doing.

Buying before confirmation is a form of anticipatory trading.

Its advantage is a lower entry price and greater potential upside.


                                               - 202 -
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 211 / 686>
Its disadvantages are a higher probability of failure, a longer waiting period, and much greater
psychological pressure.

If you choose to enter early, several conditions are essential:

       Keep the position size small.
       Define your stop-loss clearly.
       Never treat a Potential Pattern as though it were already a Confirmed Pattern.
       If the pattern fails, exit immediately.
       Never replace your original trading thesis with fundamental or emotional excuses simply
        because the trade has gone against you.

For most investors, I still recommend waiting for confirmation.

Although confirmation usually means sacrificing part of the price advantage, it provides
something far more valuable—

a clearly defined risk boundary.

Case Review

Why Did Baltic Renewables Fail?

Now let's return to Baltic Renewables.

Why did it fool Ethan?

Because it genuinely possessed several characteristics of a Head and Shoulders Bottom.

It had a left shoulder.

It had a lower head.

It appeared to be forming a right shoulder.

It even had what looked like a reasonable Neckline.

But it lacked the single most important element:

Confirmation.

First, price never broke above the Neckline on expanding Trading Volume.

Second, after breaking above the Neckline intraday, price failed to close above it.

Third, the low of the right shoulder was broken.

Fourth, that breakdown occurred on expanding Trading Volume.

Fifth, the overall sector trend remained weak.

All of those signals indicated that the Potential Head and Shoulders Bottom had never become a
Confirmed Pattern.

If Ethan had entered the trade early, he should have exited as soon as the right shoulder failed.

If he had waited for confirmation, he would never have entered the trade at all.

That is the true value of waiting for confirmation.

It does not guarantee profits.

But it prevents you from entering many trades that have not yet fully developed.

Chapter Trading Rules

That day, I asked Ethan to write down six trading rules.

First, a pattern that looks complete does not mean the pattern has actually been confirmed.

Second, a Potential Pattern is for observation, while a Confirmed Pattern is what belongs in a
trading plan.

Third, bottom reversal patterns should wait for a breakout above the Neckline, while top reversal
patterns should wait for a breakdown below the Neckline.

Fourth, Trading Volume must confirm every key breakout or breakdown.

Fifth, the quality of a chart pattern is largely determined by what happens during the retest or
rebound after the breakout or breakdown.

Sixth, every chart pattern must define its invalidation point before the trade is entered.

Then I added one more sentence.

        Chart patterns are not a game of shape recognition. They are a process of
        confirming market strength.

Ethan wrote that sentence at the very top of his notes for the chapter.

Later, he told me that this chapter completely changed the way he looked at charts.

Before, whenever he saw a pattern, he would ask:

        "Does it look like the pattern?"

Later, he began asking:

        "Has the pattern been confirmed?"

The distance between those two questions is the distance between a beginner and a professional
investor.



Chapter Summary

In this chapter, Ethan Carter learned one of the most important lessons in technical analysis:

The greatest danger of chart patterns is not failing to recognize them—it is recognizing them too
early.

When a pattern has not yet broken above the Neckline, has not been confirmed by Trading
Volume, and has not successfully passed a retest, it remains nothing more than a Potential
Opportunity.

A potential opportunity can be studied.

It can be monitored.

It can be planned for in advance.

But it must not be treated as a confirmed trend.

A high-quality chart pattern must do more than simply resemble the textbook illustration.

It must complete the confirmation process.

From this chapter onward, Ethan stopped rushing to give every chart a name.

Instead, he began breaking every chart down into a series of questions:

       Where is the key level?
       Has price broken through it?
       Has Trading Volume confirmed the move?
       Has price closed firmly beyond the key level?
       Did the retest hold?
       If the pattern fails, where do I exit?

Those questions transformed his technical analysis from guessing based on appearances into
validating market structure.
